## Abstract

Chronic damage following oncogene induction or cancer therapy can produce cellular senescence. Senescent cells not only exit the cell cycle but communicate damage signals to their environment that can trigger immune responses. Recent work has revealed that senescent tumor cells are highly immunogenic, leading to new ways to activate antitumor immunosurveillance and potentiate T cell-directed immunotherapies. However, other studies have determined that heterogeneous senescent stromal cell populations contribute to immunosuppression and tumor progression, sparking the development of senotherapeutics to target senescent cells that evade immune detection. We review current findings that provide deeper insights into the mechanisms contributing to the dichotomous role of senescence in immune modulation and how that can be leveraged for cancer immunotherapy.

### SENESCENCT CELLS ALERT IMMUNE SYSTEM TO TISSUE DAMAGE

Cellular senescence is induced by persistent genotoxic, oxidative, and/or metabolic stress that in context of carcinogenesis can be the result of aberrant oncogene activation or the biological outcome of tumor targeting therapies [1]. The original hallmarks of senescence observed in vitro were an enlarged and flattened cellular morphology and an irreversible exit from the cell cycle mediated by induction of the p16/RB and p21/p53 tumor suppressor pathways [2,3]. Through the subsequent study of cellular senescence across tissues and disease states in vivo, it is now appreciated that senescence functions beyond the cell to alert the organism of tissue damage and stimulate a multi-pronged wound healing response [4,5]. The primary means by which senescent cells attempt to restore tissue homeostasis is by activating immune surveillance. Senescent cells achieve this not only by presentation of immunomodulatory proteins on their surface, but also through a non-cell autonomous component known as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) (see glossary) mediated by transcription factors such as NF-κB, STATs, and interferon regulatory factors (IRFs) that leads to the secretion of hundreds of pleiotropic cytokines, chemokines, and other growth factors and metabolites that can impact immune cell chemotaxis, differentiation, and activity [6,7]. It has been long established that clearance of senescent pre-malignant cells by both the innate (e.g. Natural Killer (NK) cells, macrophages) and adaptive (e.g. T cells) immune system is an important tumor suppressive roadblock to malignancy [8]. Indeed, genetic knockout of perforin, an important mediator of immune cytotoxicity through its pore-forming functions, led to increased accumulation of senescent cells and rates of lymphoma development in mice [9]. Though initially tumor suppressive, senescent cell persistence and chronic SASP induction can conversely lead to inflammation and tissue damage that is immune suppressive and tumor-promoting [10]. How senescent cells eventually bypass immune surveillance mechanisms and whether they can be restored in established tumors for cancer therapy have remained outstanding questions in the field.

Recent developments using senescence reporter mice, cancer models with intact tumor-immune microenvironments, and single cell sequencing approaches have begun to shed new light on the often contrasting roles of senescence in immune activation and suppression and how this can be effectively applied to cancer treatment. First, senescent cells are now appreciated to not only persist beyond the pre-malignant phase of cancer development, but exist within other non-tumor stromal cell populations, namely macrophages and fibroblasts that are prevalent in the tumor microenvironment (TME) of solid tumors. Second, the cell type undergoing senescence, its tissue environment, and the senescence inducer (e.g. oncogene, therapy) are variables that can influence the type of SASP program elicited by senescent cells and its impact on tumor immunity. Whereas senescent tumor cells can be immunogenic and act as an adjuvant for immunotherapies by activating antigen-dependent T cell responses, senescent stromal cells in contrast generally produce a SASP the promotes the influx and polarization of suppressive myeloid cells. Finally, this newly gained knowledge of the molecular and cellular mechanisms governing immune surveillance of senescent cells, in particular the role of the cGAS/STING pathway and interferon (IFN) signaling in antigen presentation and immune checkpoint expression, is now being used to design immunotherapy strategies or alternatively develop senolytic or senomorphic approaches to mitigate detrimental senescence phenotypes that evade immune detection. Ultimately, modulation of senescent cells and their immunogenic properties have the potential to be leveraged to reactivate immune and immunotherapy responses in late-stage cancers or even for chemoprevention.

### Oncogene-induced senescence

Mutations in RAS family proteins are driver events in many cancer types. Constitutive activation of RAS leads to oncogenic induction of cellular senescence as a failsafe mechanism to block malignant transformation by both arresting damaged cells and promoting their immune clearance through the SASP [11,12] (Figure 1A; Table 1). In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), pre-malignant hepatocytes undergoing senescence following mutant NRASG12V induction can be effectively cleared by both macrophages and antigen-dependent CD4+ T helper (Th1) cells through production of SASP chemokines (CCL2, CCL5) and cytokines (IL-1α) to prevent progression to malignancy [8]. p21, canonically known for its cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) inhibitor functions and role in senescence-mediated growth arrest, can also promote Retinoblastoma (RB) protein complexes with SMAD and STAT transcription factors to activate the inflammatory SASP [13]. This p21-driven SASP leads to production of chemokines such as CXCL14 that attract activated M1 macrophages and in turn cytotoxic CD8+ T cells to clear senescent KRASG12V hepatocytes. Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) activity is also necessary for senescence and SASP induction of IL-1α/β and subsequent macrophage-mediated tumor suppression in KRAS-driven lung cancer [14,15]. In the setting of Pten-loss induced senescence, genetic deletion of JUN or STAT3, which are transcriptional regulators of the inflammatory SASP, reduced macrophage and neutrophil infiltration, leading to accelerated prostate tumor growth [16,17]. Furthermore, sexual dimorphism has been found to play a role in SASP-mediated immune responses in WNT pathway-driven adrenal tumorigenesis. Males produced a more robust SASP and immune surveillance by macrophages, neutrophils and dendritic cells (DCs) that led to regressions following Znfr3 knockout-mediated hyperplasia induction and a lower incidence of metastasis as compared to female counterparts [18].

Still, emerging evidence suggests that the timing of senescence and the ability of the immune system to efficiently remove senescent cells is critical for its tumor suppressive functions (Figure 1B; Table 1). Indeed, while CCL2 production from senescent hepatocytes attracts phagocytic macrophages to promote their clearance in the pre-malignant phase, a follow-up study found that persistent senescent cells that avoid immune detection and continue to chronically produce CCL2 attract immature myeloid cells (e.g. myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs)) that inhibit effective NK cell immune surveillance in established HCC [19]. In another study, sustained p53 activation in KRAS mutant hepatocytes following MDM2 deletion led to a chronic inflammatory TME through the SASP that accelerated HCC development [20]. Moreover, functional experiments to pharmacogenetically ablate p16+ senescent cells using mice harboring a p16-3MR allele have demonstrated that in late-stage glioblastoma (GBM), removal of malignant cells maintaining a senescent phenotype can increase survival and shift immunity away from a macrophage and toward a T cell response [21]. These recent revelations reinforce the concept that the immune stimulatory mechanisms of the SASP are tumor suppressive only when an acute and orchestrated innate and adaptive immune response following SASP induction to fully clear senescent cells can be achieved.

### Therapy-induced senescence with DNA damaging agents

Following an initial wave of cell death, senescence is a common biological outcome of conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and other DNA damaging agents that induce double-stranded breaks and a prolonged DNA damage response (DDR) [22]. DNA damage links senescence-associated cell cycle arrest with the SASP through activation of the cytoplasmic DNA sensor cGAS. Release of damaged double-stranded DNA following dissolution of the nuclear envelope or mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization is sensed by cGAS, which then produces a secondary messenger (cGAMP) that activates STING. STING can then translocate from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) to the Golgi, where it signals through a downstream cascade to activate IRF3 to stimulate Type I IFN genes, as well as NF-κB to induce additional pro-inflammatory SASP factors [23-25].

The SASP elicited from senescent tumor cells following treatment with DNA damaging cancer therapies can reactivate anti-tumor immunity in immunologically “cold” tumor types (Figure 1A; Table 1). Cisplatin and PARP inhibitors induce senescence and downstream cGAS/STING signaling that enhance anti-tumor NK and CD8+ T cell immunity and overall treatment outcomes in homologous recombination (HR)-deficient ovarian and colorectal cancer models through the SASP factors CCL5 and CXCL10 [26,27]. Radiation is also able to induce a SASP and release extracellular vesicles (EVs) containing DNA:RNA hybrids and LINE-1 retrotransposons that can activate cGAS/STING signaling to polarize inflammatory macrophages and mediate regression of non-irradiated tumors through the abscopal effect [28]. Aurora Kinase inhibitors that induce senescence and DNA damage through blocking mitosis can promote CD8+ T cell immunity and synergize with anti-CTLA-4 immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) in a p21-dependent manner in melanoma [29]. Though the on-target effects of therapy-induced senescence on tumor cells can enhance anti-tumor immune surveillance, it is important to consider that senescent tumor cells that are not cleared, as well as the off-target effects of these therapies on fibroblast and immune senescence in the TME and systemically, can promote chronic inflammation that contributes to disease relapse and progression [30-32] (Figure 1B; Table 1).

### Therapy-induced senescence with CDK4/6 inhibitors

FDA-approved CDK4/6 kinase inhibitors that mimic p16 function derepress RB to induce G1/S phase arrest and senescence across different cancer contexts without eliciting DNA damage [33] (Figure 1A; Table 1). Though CDK4/6 inhibition alone is generally not sufficient to induce a SASP [34,35], combining the CDK4/6 inhibitor palbociclib with the MEK inhibitor trametinib, which blocks MAPK signaling downstream of RAS activation, can elicit a robust SASP in KRAS mutant cancers. Trametinib and palbociclib (T/P) combined to induce RB-mediated senescence and an NF-κB-driven pro-inflammatory SASP containing cytokines TNF-α and IL-15 in tumor cells that resulted in NK cell infiltration and anti-tumor immune surveillance to control the growth of KRAS mutant lung cancer [36]. In contrast, in models of KRAS mutant pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), T/P induced a SASP rich in angiogenic factors such VEGFs, PDGFs, and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) that increase vascularization, as well as cytokines and chemokines like IL-6, CXCL1, and CCL5 that promoted endothelial activation. Though this led to increased trafficking of CD8+ T cell from blood vessels into the PDAC TME, it was not sufficient to block tumor progression or stimulate anti-tumor immune responses [37]. Unlike lung tumors, pancreatic tumors displayed transcriptional repression and reduced chromatin accessibility of SASP genes mediated by the histone methyltransferase EZH2. Tumor-intrinsic EZH2 inhibition enhanced pro-inflammatory SASP production of chemokines CCL2 and CXCL9/10 and led to NK and T cell-mediated tumor control and even complete responses in preclinical PDAC mouse models [38]. Combining T/P with lipid nanoparticle (LNP) delivery of STING and TLR4 agonists that activate downstream IFN signaling also led to synergistic induction of pro-inflammatory SASP factors, increased antigen presentation by DCs and tumor cells, and Type I IFN receptor (IFNAR)-dependent CD8+ T cell responses against PDAC [39]. These findings suggest that there may exist tissue-specific differences in the SASP output that could explain its contrasting effects on tumor and immune responses across cancer types. Moreover, boosting the pro-inflammatory SASP following therapy-induced senescence through (i.) EZH2 blockade or (ii.) STING/TLR4 agonism can be an effective means to potentiate innate and adaptive immune control in PDAC (Figure 2A).

### IMMUNE STIMULATORY RECEPTORS AND LIGANDS ON SENESCENT TUMOR CELLS ENHANCE CANCER IMMUNOGENECITY

Senescent tumor cells upregulate immune stimulatory receptors and ligands on their surface that prompt immune recognition and clearance (Figure 1A; Table 1). IFN and NF-κB signaling downstream of DNA damage and cGAS/STING converge to induce expression of stimulatory NK cell ligands that bind the activating NK cell receptor NKG2D (RAE-1, MULT-1, and H60 in mice, and MICA/B and ULBP1-6 in humans) [40]. NKG2D ligands, as well as the adhesion molecule ICAM-1 that is important for mediating tumor-immune synapses, are necessary for NK cell engagement and secretion of cytolytic granules that can kill senescent cells [36].

Major histocompatibility complex-I (MHC-I) can also be induced on senescent cells across different cancer contexts. MHC-I is responsible for presenting antigens on the cell surface that can be recognized by specific T cell receptors (TCRs) on CD8+ T cells to generate antigen-driven T cell recognition and cytotoxicity. Therapy-induced senescence with T/P could stimulate expression of MHC-I and other genes important for antigen presentation/processing in senescent tumors cells that were further enhanced by EZH2 inhibition or STING agonism in PDAC models [37-39]. In the setting of p53-mediated senescence in liver cancer, senescent cells remodel their surface proteome to express the IFNγ receptor (IFNGR1), allowing them to respond to IFNγ produced by immune cells in the TME. This response, in turn, activates downstream antigen presentation machinery that is necessary for CD8+ T cell surveillance independent of the SASP [41]. Similarly, in multiple models of therapy-induced senescence, Type I IFN signaling through secretion of SASP factors IFNα/β could induce MHC-I expression and alter the immunopeptidome to initiate CD8+ T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity against senescence-specific antigens [42]. These findings demonstrate that senescence can lead to increased immunogenicity in cancer, and suggest that senescent cells could serve as immune adjuvants or cellular vaccines themselves.

### SENESCENT TUMOR CELLS AS VACCINES AND ADJUVANTS

The concept that the immunogenic properties of senescent cells could serve as a cancer vaccine was first tested over a decade ago by Meng and colleagues, where they demonstrated that transplantation of B16 murine melanoma cells induced to senescence following PARP inhibitor treatment and irradiation could augment the effects of ionizing radiation and clear established tumors through mediating antigen-dependent T cell responses [43] (Figure 2A). Similar results were recently shown through immunization of mice with doxorubicin-treated senescent B16 melanoma cells, which could activate macrophage and T cell responses and block melanoma development as a prophylactic measure [42]. Intraperitoneal inoculation of senescent ovarian cancer cells pre-treated with cisplatin to induce senescence and the topoisomerase 1 (TOP1) inhibitor irinotecan to further boost a cGAS/STING-driven SASP in mice could also activate immune responses against ovarian cancer [44]. It is important to point out that dendritic cells (DCs), professional antigen-presenting cells required for efficient antigen presentation to CD4+ T cells via MHC-II and CD8+ T cells via MHC-I molecules, were also expanded and activated and likely contributed as adjuvants for these senescent cell vaccination approaches [42,44]. Indeed, transplantation of DCs ex vivo primed with senescent CT26 cancer cells into tumor-bearing animals treated with irradiation or anti-PD-L1 ICB produced STING-dependent immunological memory that blocked both primary tumor growth and metastatic dissemination [45]. Isolation of senescent cancer cell-derived nanovesicles containing IFNγ and TNF-α and their subsequent adoptive transfer also primed DCs in the lymph nodes and anti-tumor T cell responses in melanoma models in combination with an antigen vaccine [46]. As such, senescence induction could be a means to further enhance DC or neo-antigen vaccine approaches that are being tested in cancer patients in the clinic [47]. Collectively, these results in preclinical models suggest that immunization with senescent cells or their products can be an effective approach to not only immunologically block oncogenesis or treat established tumors and metastases, but also enhance T cell priming to potentiate existing immunotherapy modalities.

### INHIBITORY CHECKPOINTS ON SENESCENT TUMOR CELLS LEAD TO ICB SENSITIVITY

Senescent cells also upregulate a number of immune checkpoints that can lead to eventual lymphocyte exhaustion and dysfunction (Figure 1; Table 1). A collection of recent studies in aging and cancer have shown that the immune checkpoint PD-L1 is upregulated on senescent cells through both the SASP that drives JAK/STAT signaling and PD-L1 transcription, as well as p16-mediated inhibition of CDK4/6 that blocks proteasomal degradation of PD-L1 protein [48-50]. Binding of PD-L1 on senescent cells to its receptor PD-1 expressed on immune cells allows for senescent cells to evade immune-mediated destruction. Simultaneously, this also creates an induced vulnerability to anti-PD-1 or PD-L1 ICB. For example, T/P-induced senescence leads to induction of PD-L1 on senescent tumor cells and PD-1 on infiltrating CD8+ T cells, and combined treatment with anti-PD-1 ICB can reactivate cytotoxic T cell immunity and tumor responses in PDAC [37]. Additional studies have shown that therapy-induced senescence with chemotherapy, radiation, CDK4/6 inhibitor, PARP inhibitor, or tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) regimens can sensitize “cold” tumor types such as breast, ovarian, pancreas, and colorectal cancer that typically do not respond to immunotherapy to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 ICB to achieve potent anti-tumor efficacy [26,27,44,51,52]. Moreover, expression of senescence and SASP genes have also been associated with better anti-PD-1/PD-L1 ICB clinical outcomes in “hot” melanoma and renal cell carcinoma (RCC) [53-55].

PD-L2, an alternative PD-1 ligand that is poorly expressed in cancer, was also specifically upregulated across models of therapy-induced senescence through NF-κB-mediated SASP signaling [56]. PD-L2 expression was associated with elevated levels of SASP chemokines CXCL1/2 that attracted MDSCs. PD-L2 blockade in combination with doxorubicin could inhibit MDSC infiltration and activate CD8+ T cells to mediate tumor control [56]. Another study found that senescent breast tumor cells expressed multiple immune checkpoints following doxorubicin chemotherapy, including not just PD-L1 but also GALECTIN-9 and CD80 that bind inhibitory receptors TIM-3 and CTLA-4 on immune cells, respectively [57]. Single cell analysis revealed that these immune checkpoint molecules were expressed on distinct populations of senescent cells. Whereas one population expressed high levels of PD-L1 and GALECTIN-9 driven by IFNγ and increased chromatin accessibility of IRF1 binding motifs, another population expressed high amounts of CD80 and CD86 activated by p53 and NF-κB transcriptional activity independent of IFN signaling. Combining anti-CTLA-4 with anti-PD-1 antibodies further enhanced breast cancer responses to doxorubicin-induced senescence [57], indicating that multiple immune checkpoints on heterogenous senescent cell populations may need to be targeted for their full immune clearance.

Chronic SASP production can also lead to increased expression of NK cell inhibitory ligands on the surface of senescent cells. HLA-E, which binds the inhibitory receptor NKG2A expressed on NK and CD8+ T cells, is induced on the surface on senescent melanocytic nevi through a p38MAPK-driven SASP and IL-6 signaling, contributing to their ability to persist and evade immune detection [58]. MMPs secreted through the SASP following RAS- or therapy-induced senescence can also cleave activating NKG2D ligands from the surface of senescent cells to further prevent NK cell clearance [59]. The NKG2A antibody monalizumab, as well as MMP2 and MMP9 inhibitors, are currently in clinical development, and could serve as alternative approaches to resensitize senescent cells to immune clearance in both developing and established tumors.

### SENESCENT STROMAL CELLS PROMOTE IMMUNE SUPPRESSION IN THE TME

Senescence following oncogene induction or cancer therapy occurs within both pre-malignant and malignant tumor cells as well as non-tumor stromal cells including fibroblasts, myeloid cells, and endothelial cells that are major constituents of the TME (Table 1). Stromal senescence has been shown to promote a chronic inflammatory TME and MDSC-mediated immune suppression through factors such as IL-6 that inhibit effective T cell responses against cancer [60,61]. Recently, a number of seminal studies utilizing p16 reporter models to track and deplete senescent cells and single cell sequencing approaches to characterize different senescent cell populations have further revealed the tumor-promoting and immune suppressive functions of senescent fibroblasts and myeloid cells (Figure 1B; Table 1).

### Myeloid cells

Crossing models of KRAS mutant lung cancer with INK-ATTAC and p16-FDR mice to track and deplete p16+ senescent cells demonstrated that, surprisingly, senescent cells were most prominent in the immune compartment following oncogene-induced senescence [62,63]. Single cell RNA-sequencing analysis of mouse lung lesions revealed these senescent immune cells to be predominantly CXCR1+ alveolar macrophages [63] or FOLR2+/CD163+ interstitial macrophages [62] with immune suppressive features. These same senescent macrophage populations were also observed in early lung cancer patient samples. Pharmacogenetic depletion of p16+ cells or senolytic treatment using BCL-2 inhibitors could remove senescent macrophages, delay lung tumorigenesis, and remodel the immune suppressive TME to increase cytotoxic CD8+ T cell numbers in mice. Other myeloid subsets such as MDSCs/neutrophils were shown to express the senescence markers p16, p21, and senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) and have a heightened persistence in the TME in a Pten-deficient prostate cancer model despite their short half-life [64]. Mechanistically, APOE secreted by prostate tumor cells could activate senescence in neutrophils by binding TREM2. TREM2+ senescent neutrophils were more suppressive of NK and T cell activity and tumor-promoting compared to non-senescent neutrophils. Collectively, these works demonstrate that myeloid cells can express senescence markers during oncogene-induced senescence and promote an immune suppressive environment that contributes to tumor development and progression. Still, the mechanisms by which myeloid cells become senescent during tumorigenesis, how senescence impacts their functions, and specific senolytic targets to remove them will require further investigation.

### Fibroblasts

One of the earliest studies of cellular senescence in vivo showed that co-transplanting senescent fibroblasts with pre-malignant and malignant mammary epithelial cells or epidermal keratinocytes increased the rate of tumorigenesis and degree of tumor progression in immunodeficient mice [65]. Recent studies have started to identify senescent cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) naturally occurring in the TME and elucidate their immune suppressive functions as a mechanism driving tumor promotion (Figure 1B; Table 1). Single cell sequencing and multiplexed immunostaining revealed a subset of myofibroblasts (myCAFs) that arise and persist during the course of murine and human pancreatic [66] and breast cancer development [67]. In PDAC, senescent myCAFs were maintained by FAK signaling and secreted an extracellular matrix (ECM)-rich SASP including factors such as MMPs and TGF-β that could modify the TME in an immunosuppressive manner.

Crossing p48-Cre; LSL-KRASG12D;p53fl/fl (KPC) mice that develop spontaneous PDAC with INK-ATTAC mice to pharmacogenetically deplete p16+ senescent CAFs, or treating KPC mice with senolytic BCL-2 family inhibitor ABT-263, reduced fibrosis and immune suppressive functions of macrophages and increased antigen presentation by DCs, leading to increased numbers and activation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and delayed tumor progression [66]. Another study using PDAC mouse models found that senescent CAFs created an immune suppressive TME through secretion of inflammatory SASP factors such as IL-6 and CXCL2, and that genetic or pharmacological senolytic approaches could increase CD8+ T cell activity and potentiate immunotherapy with a combination of CTLA-4 and PD-1 blocking and CD40 agonist antibodies [68]. In breast cancer, senescent myCAFs were marked by NCAM and also secreted an ECM-rich SASP. Senescent myCAF depletion using BCL-2 family inhibitor ABT-737 or by crossing the MMTV-PyMT breast cancer model with INK-ATTAC mice reduced tumor growth not through impacting T cells but rather by increasing infiltration and activation of NK cells that are normally inhibited by ECM factors [67], suggesting variability in immune mechanisms across tissues and disease models.

Senescence can also occur in fibroblasts as an off-target effect of cancer therapies and contribute to an immune suppressive TME fostering therapy resistance (Figure 1B; Table 1). Another subset of CAFs, known as inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs), can undergo IL-1α and p53-mediated senescence and SASP production in irradiated rectal tumors [69]. Subsequent senolytic ABT-199 treatment could target senescent iCAFs, reverse radioresistance and metastatic progression, and increase CD8+ T cell infiltration in the TME. Likewise, the topoisomerase inhibitor mitoxantrone was revealed to induce fibroblast senescence and the growth factor AREG as part of the SASP in prostate cancer [70]. Binding of AREG produced by senescent fibroblasts to its receptor EGFR on tumor cells subsequently led to PD-L1 upregulation and tumor immune evasion. Though this latter work uncovers a mechanism by which senescent fibroblasts can cross-talk with tumor cells through the SASP to indirectly enable immune suppression, follow-up studies are needed to better understand how senescent fibroblasts communicate directly with different immune cell types in the TME.

The field has just started to develop transgenic mice where senescence can be induced, traced, and ablated in specific cell lineages [71,72]. Such models will provide further granularity not only on distinctions between tumor and stromal senescence, but also within different stromal cell populations. For example, vascular endothelial cells can undergo senescence in the lung and liver TME and have different functions compared to other stromal senescent cells like macrophages [62,72]. Indeed, it has recently been shown that senescent endothelial cells can contribute to anti-tumor immunity, vascular integrity, and prevention of fibrosis in the liver [73,74].

### LEVERAGING SENOTHERAPEUTICS TO CLEAR IMMUNE EVASIVE SENESCENT CELLS AND SASP PROGRAMS

In addition to eliminating senescent cells that evade immune destruction through ICB approaches (Figure 2B), another strategy has been the use of senotherapeutics, including senolytic agents to directly kill senescent cells (Figure 2C) or senomorphic agents to block or reprogram immune suppressive and tumor-promoting SASP programs (Figure 2D) [75]. Recent work has demonstrated that senotherapies in certain contexts can also reactivate anti-tumor immune responses and enhance immunotherapy outcomes in cancer.

### Senolytics

Senolytic strategies have primarily been focused on targeting pro-survival and anti-apoptotic pathways engaged in senescent cells that allow them to persist (Figure 2C). The senolytic combination of the tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), dasatinib and natural flavonoid, quercetin that target pro-survival PI3K/AKT signaling has entered clinical trials for a number of aging-related diseases and is showing promising results [76]. In cancer, the most tested senolytic strategy has been the use of BCL-2 family inhibitors (e.g. ABT-199, −263, −737) as a means to induce apoptosis in senescent cells that upregulate anti-apoptotic proteins BCL-2, BCL-W, and/or BCL-xL for their survival. These compounds can clear tumor-promoting senescent cells as a means to block tumor onset and progression, and remodel the immune suppressive landscape of tumors to potentiate immunotherapy [21,62,63,66-68]. In the setting of stromal senescence following total-body irradiation or doxorubicin treatment, ABT-263 treatment could reverse monocyte-derived immune suppression and reactivate CD8+ T cell immunity to restore anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy efficacy in colorectal tumors and lymphomas [77].

Still, recent developments suggest that senescent tumor cells may not be uniformly sensitive to BCL-2 family antagonists and may be dependent on other anti-apoptotic proteins, including MCL-1 [78,79]. The MCL-1 inhibitor S63845 had superior effects compared to ABT-263 in terms of clearing senescent cells and blocking distant metastases in prostate cancer models [80]. Treatment was also accompanied by a reduction in infiltrating MDSCs and macrophages and an increase in cytotoxicity markers in NK and T cells. Screening approaches to identify therapies that can induce senescence and senolytic agents to then target vulnerabilities acquired in senescent cells as a one-two punch has uncovered a number of new senolytic strategies in liver cancer. CDC7 inhibition was shown to induce senescence specifically in liver tumor cells with mutations in p53, and the addition of the mTOR inhibitor AZD8055 could then kill senescent tumor cells that were not fully cleared by the immune system in vivo [81]. Unique cell surface marker expression on senescent tumor cells has also been leveraged for innovative Chimeric Antigen Receptor (CAR)-T cell senolytic approaches. uPAR was identified as a conserved cell surface marker across different senescent conditions, and uPAR-specific CAR-T cells could efficiently remove senescent lung tumor cells after T/P treatment [82]. Such screening and comparative transcriptomic methodologies across senescence models will continue to inform new and likely more specific senescence markers that could be targeted with small molecule or cell-based engineering approaches to clear detrimental senescent cells that evade immune detection.

### Senomorphics

Chronic SASP expression or a shift to pro-tumorigenic SASP programs can lead to upregulation of immune checkpoints and immune suppression mediated by myeloid cells that prevent effective immune surveillance of senescent cells. As such, an alternative strategy to senolytic approaches has been the use of senomorphics that target the transcriptional regulators of the SASP or specific SASP factors themselves while leaving the cell autonomous tumor suppressive functions of senescent cells intact (Figure 2D). P38MAPK is induced by oxidative stress in senescent cells and sustains the SASP by enhancing NF-κB transcriptional activity and SASP mRNA transcript stability [83]. P38MAPK signaling is heightened in senescent stromal cells, where it inhibits STING-driven interferon signaling but maintains an immune suppressive SASP program through cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-8 and the inhibitory receptor HLA-E [23,58,84], making it a promising candidate for senomorphic approaches. Indeed, inhibition of stromal p38a was recently shown to shift macrophages toward a tumor-suppressive M1 phenotype, trigger CD4+ T cell responses, and synergize with OX40 agonist immunotherapy to reduce metastatic breast cancer growth [85].

Another recent senomorphic strategy has been to target specific SASP factors, particularly those responsible for myeloid-mediated immune suppression. Blocking CXCR2 expressed on myeloid cells that is activated by ligands CXCL1/2 and IL-8 secreted by senescent prostate tumor cells through the SASP could reduce myeloid cell chemotaxis and confer durable clinical benefits in combination with standard-of-care enzalutamide treatment in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) patients [86]. Moreover, IL-1α and IL-1β regulate the late arm of the SASP and reinforce NF-κB signaling through IL-1R, and subsequent IL-1α inactivation could delay pancreatic cancer progression and reduce macrophage infiltration in mice [87]. Similar senomorphic approaches could be tested with IL-1R and IL-6/IL-6R antagonists currently in clinical development for cancer and approved for treating rheumatoid arthritis.

A complementary approach to SASP blockade could be to reprogram the SASP output from one that is tumor-promoting and immune suppressive to one that is immune stimulatory. Indeed, treatment with a retinoic acid receptor (RAR) agonist adapalene can block the activity of pioneer transcription factor AP-1 following docetaxel-induced senescence in prostate cancer cells and remodel the SASP from a tumor-promoting (e.g. IL-6, MMPs) to an immune stimulatory (e.g. IL-12,-15,-33) program [88]. The combination of docetaxel and adapalene also led to upregulation of activating NK cell ligands on senescent cells and effective anti-tumor NK cell immunity in prostate cancer models. In the future, approaches to enhance cGAS/STING and downstream interferon signaling [39] (Figure 2A) may also be an effective senomorphic strategy to reprogram the SASP to promote anti-tumor immunity and immunotherapy efficacy.

The timing and type of SASP programs (e.g. pro-inflammatory, anti-inflammatory, ECM remodeling, angiogenic) induced and immune responses elicited likely dictate whether senolytic or senomorphic agents or conversely ICB immunotherapies should be implemented in different senescent cancer contexts (see
Outstanding Questions). Given that cGAS/STING, as well as the NLRP3-driven inflammasome [89], are induced in senescent cells and can also regulate immunogenic cell death pathways such as pyroptosis [90], one possibility is whether inducing immunogenic cell death in senescent cells by manipulating these pathways could simultaneously produce acute immune stimulation while still removing detrimental senescent cells and their SASP. Engineering tools to deliver senescence-inducing agents and senotherapies to specific cellular compartments in the TME (e.g. nanoparticles, antibody-drug conjugates) may also help to better activate immune stimulatory senescence programs while limiting pro-tumorigenic senescent cells.

### CONCLUDING REMARKS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Senescent cells, through both cell autonomous (immunomodulatory cell surface molecules) and cell non-autonomous (SASP) properties, are in many ways immunogenic and primed for immune clearance. As such, senescence can be harnessed for immune cell infiltration, activation, and anti-tumor cytotoxicity to block tumor initiation, augment therapy outcomes, and eradicate established cancer, including using senescent cells themselves as vaccines or adjuvants (Figure 1A; Table 1). Still, because of the chronic and ever evolving nature of tumors, persistent senescent cells, particularly stromal cells, present multiple immune checkpoints to block innate and adaptive immune surveillance and produce an anti-inflammatory and tumor-promoting SASP leading to lymphocyte exhaustion, myeloid-mediated immune suppression, and tumor progression (Figure 1B; Table 1). The mechanisms by which senescent cells evade the immune system create vulnerabilities to existing immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) immunotherapies, as well as new-age senotherapies that target anti-apoptotic or inflammatory properties of senescent cells (Figure 2). Understanding the senescent cell types and SASPs generated by different classes of senescence-inducing agents will allow for the development of rational combination therapies to fully harness the anti-tumorigenic and immune stimulatory properties of senescence for cancer treatment.

Recent results in preclinical tumor models and clinical trials demonstrate that inducing senescence or modulating senescent cells with senotherapies could lead to promising anti-tumor effects. Clinical data that integrates senescence marker expression, SASP outputs, and immune profiles with tumor and survival outcomes is still needed to understand how senescence can best be leveraged as a biomarker for precision medicine and for effectively implementing cancer immunotherapy in patients. A major roadblock to achieving this clinical goal is the lack of specific markers to universally identify senescent cells in cancer. Single cell sequencing efforts have only further unveiled the vast degree of heterogeneity in senescent marker expression across cell types, organs, and disease conditions [91,92]. Going forward, we will likely need to define specific senescence markers for each tumor and stromal cell type within the TME of that cancer. Classifying appropriate senescence markers will also be critical in designing effective senolytic strategies, as current senolytic agents target pro-survival and cell death pathways also expressed in growing tumor cells as well as normal hematopoietic cells, leading to off-target effects and toxicity [93]. Next-generation spatial transcriptomic and machine learning approaches will facilitate the identification of heterogeneous senescent cells based on not just marker expression but also spatial localization and morphology [94,95]. Spatial transcriptomics in particular will be critical for defining various tumor and stromal senescent cell populations and how they differ in their proximity and interactions with immune cells, as well as unique biomarkers that could be leveraged for senotherapeutics to remove only detrimental senescent cells while leaving anti-tumorigenic and immune stimulatory senescent cell populations intact.

Cellular senescence, derived from Senex in Latin meaning “old”, was first described by Hayflick and Moorhead in the 1960’s as a cell culture aging phenomenon [2]. Since then, much our understanding of the role of senescence in disease and immunity has come from aging-related studies. Given the risk of developing most cancers increases dramatically with age and the many overlaps between the “hallmarks of aging”[96] and “hallmarks of cancer”[97], it will be important to study the impact of age-related senescence on cancer development and immune surveillance. The aging immune system, or immunosenescence, can impact the cytotoxic functions of T cells and myeloid phenotypes [62,63,98], which could contribute to the accumulation of senescent cells with age that are not cleared by the immune system. Understanding the mechanisms by which stromal cells, including immune cells, become senescent during aging, and how this impacts their functions in cancer, are critical future directions.
