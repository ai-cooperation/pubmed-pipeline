## Abstract

Immunotherapy, including immune checkpoint inhibition, has transformed the prognosis of many patients with cancer. However, most patients have primary or secondary resistant tumors to currently available cancer immunotherapies. Changes in glycosylation of malignant cells and in the tumor microenvironment are a new target to overcome resistance to current cancer immunotherapies and to improve the outcome of patients. Here, we summarize how changes in glycosylation in cancer have functional consequences on the immune system. Such changes can be directly targeted with drugs. Moreover, they can mediate immune-suppressive effects. For example, increased sialylation can enhance interactions with immune-inhibitory Siglec receptors, and galectin-mediated interactions can modulate immune responses in the context of cancer. Finally, we provide an overview of approaches to therapeutically target these changes for the improvement of cancer immunotherapy.

### Introduction

Cancer therapy has been revolutionized by therapeutics that inhibit immune checkpoints to harness the power of the immune system in fighting cancer. Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) therapies have also emerged as a powerful tool for hematologic malignancies.1 The use of immune cells in cancer treatment has become a central focus for engaging the immune system in the fight against cancer.

In the tumor microenvironment (TME), intricate immunosuppressive signals are established in the crosstalk between cancer cells and immune cells that result in a differentiation trajectory towards exhaustion and the suppression of tumor-fighting immune cells.2 3 Successful tumors use a broad range of pathways to aid in immune evasion, often by expressing inhibitory ligands and receptors that foster the creation of immunosuppressive networks favoring tumor growth and progression. The discovery of the inhibitory immunological pathways underlying cancer immune evasion laid the foundation to the immunotherapeutic strategies based on immune-checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) targeting programmed cell death protein-1 or cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4, which have revolutionized cancer treatment4 and was also the basis for the 2018 Nobel Prize.5 However, immunotherapies are not yet effective in solid tumors and still only a small proportion of patients respond. The identification of the mechanisms underlying a suppressive TME and the players that contribute to cancer immune evasion is leading to the discoveries of novel immunotherapeutic modalities. The cancer glycome constitutes a central axis for immune modulation that has been gaining high relevance in cancer research with promising applications in cancer immunotherapy.6

During cancer development and progression, significant changes in the glycosylation profile of the TME occur, as the result of the enzymatic addition of carbohydrate structures (glycans) to essentially all cells, including cancer cells, stromal cells, and immune cells (table 1). Changes in cellular glycosylation are a hallmark of cancer and tumor-associated glycans participate in each pathophysiological step of tumor development and progression.7 The cancer glycome is composed of a vast array of altered and aberrant glycan structures, including an overall increased sialylation, the overexpression of complex branched N-glycans, hyper-fucosylation, as well as abnormal expression of truncated O-glycans, such as the Tn antigen, which overall modify the TME even at premalignant stages.8 These tumor-associated glycans establish powerful glyco-immunological networks that contribute to imposing immunosuppressive networks and immune evasion mechanisms. Cancer glycosylation is a central axis in tumor immune editing by contributing to a self-associated molecular pattern9 that can be sensed and recognized by a variety of glycan binding proteins (GBP), including Siglecs, galectins, and C-type lectins, expressed and secreted by a variety of immune cells in the TME.1014 Recent studies have demonstrated that aberrant cellular glycosylation in the TME can be an early event of malignant transformation, potentially defining the risk for cancer progression.15

Recent advances in glycomics and glycoproteomics techniques1618 have led to significant discoveries in glycans profiling and spatial cancer glycome characterization that have been illuminating the biological contribution of glycans and GBP in cancer immune-editing, providing the identification of new biomarkers and glycan-based targets for new immunotherapeutic strategies. In particular, changes in serum protein glycosylation have been associated with resistance to current immune checkpoint inhibitors, although not all mechanisms have been elucidated to date. Altered carbohydrate structures are the basis for pathological interactions mediating immune suppression for cancer and are direct potential targets for antibody-directed therapeutic approaches.

The powerful immunomodulatory properties of glycans and GBP in the TME highlight the importance of understanding and cracking the “cancer sugar-code”, which holds significant promise in advancing immune-oncology and modern cancer immunotherapy. Recent breakthrough discoveries in cancer glyco-immunology are bringing to light the design of innovative biotherapeutic molecules and antibodies targeting tumor-associated glycans, including anti-glycan CAR-T cells as well as targeting tumor GBP, such as Siglecs and galectins,1921 which poses a significant promise for the future impact of GlycoMedicine in oncology and in cancer immunotherapy.

In this review, we discuss recent advances, current challenges, and prospects in the power of the glycan-GBP immune axis for revealing novel mechanisms underlying cancer immune evasion and a suppressive TME. We also discuss potential novel glycan-based therapeutic targets that have been exploited to improve the efficacy of immunotherapy, single or in combination with conventional ICI.

### Glycan biosynthesis

Biosynthesis of proteins follows the central dogma in biology, wherein a gene acts as a temple for the sequence of a protein. Alternative messenger RNA splicing and post-translational modifications greatly add to this diversity of molecular structures, but there is still a finite number of relevant structures. Glycan biosynthesis differs significantly from protein and DNA/RNA biosynthesis in two ways: (1) glycans are not built from a template but rather from the spatiotemporal control of glycosyltransferases, their nucleotide sugar donors, and their acceptors within the secretory pathway and (2) glycans can branch, which exponentially adds to the number of potential structural isomers. Approximately 1% of the human genome is dedicated to encoding glycosyltransferase.22 These act to assemble at least 15 different classes of glycans, all from nine monosaccharide building blocks in humans. As for the acceptors, these can be protein carriers (both membrane-bound or soluble) and glycolipids.23 Alterations in glycan biosynthesis are frequently found in cancer19 and will be discussed in detail below.

### Glycan-binding proteins

Glycans impart many intrinsic properties to proteins, the most understood being the stabilizing effect imparted to the three-dimensional fold of proteins by complex N-glycans.24 However, glycans also provide extrinsic effects to the proteins to which they are attached through serving as ligands for GBPs. Indeed, the human genome encodes a plethora of GBPs, with 215 characterized to date.25 Many of these GBPs can be categorized into families, with the four major ones being: I-type lectin (sialic acid-binding immunoglobulin-type lectins; Siglecs), C-type lectins, galectins, and the P-type lectins. As the first three families can all recognize cell surface glycans, they can induce biological effects that stem from the changes in glycans in cancer (figure 1). In the next section, we dive deeper into key functions of these three GBP proteins, particularly as it pertains to their ability to skew immune cell function in the context of cancer.

### Sialic acid-Siglec axis

Siglecs are a family of rapidly evolving cell surface GBPs that are predominantly expressed on immune cells. The ligands of Siglecs are sialoglycans—that is, glycans capped by sialic acid on the non-reducing end.26 As one of the nine basic monosaccharide building blocks, sialic acid has a number of unique properties compared with other monosaccharides, the most relevant of which is its negative charge from the carboxylic acid and its physical location at the tip of glycans.27 This location places significant evolutionary pressure on sialic acid in host-pathogen interactions, and indeed, many human pathogens (bacteria and virus) have lectins of their own that recognize sialoglycans for cell entry and infection.27 Accordingly, this evolutionary pressure leads to changes in sialic acid itself, which subsequently requires changes to Siglecs, leading to significant differences for Siglecs between species.28 In humans, there are 15 Siglec genes, with some individuals having fewer due to polymorphisms in Siglec-14 and Siglec-16.29 The family can be subdivided in two different ways. The first is based on the immunomodulatory motif present in their cytoplasmic tail. Many Siglec receptors are inhibitory as most have immunotyrosine-based inhibitory (ITIM) or switch (ITSM) motifs.26 Siglec-1 and Siglec-4 do not have ITIMs but have other immunomodulatory roles. Siglec-14, Siglec-15, and Siglec-16 lack an ITIM or ITSM and, instead, have a positively charged amino acid in their transmembrane segment that causes them to pair with DAP-10 or DAP-12, which themselves are activatory.30 The second way to subcategorize Siglecs is based on how conserved their sequence orthologs are between species. The CD22-related Siglecs (Siglec-1/CD169, Siglec-2/CD22, Siglec-4/MAG, and Siglec-15) are relatively well conserved and, consequently, have a similar expression pattern, ligands, and function.31 The rest are CD33-related Siglecs and are much more diverse between species, which corresponds to divergence in expression pattern, glycan ligands, and function.32

Interactions of Siglecs with their glycan ligands control their proximity to other critical immunomodulatory receptors, allowing Siglecs to modulate immune cell activation. These sialoglycans can be on the same cell (cis) or neighbor cell (trans).26 A leading hypothesis is that Siglec-sialoglycan interactions are a form of “self” recognition to prevent inadvertent autoimmune responses.33 However, this mechanism can be exploited by cancer. Many cancers overexpress sialoglycan ligands of Siglecs, which is thought to be a way to engage inhibitory Siglecs and thwart antitumorous immunity.14 34 Many examples now exist for such mechanisms in relevance to T cells,3537 natural killer (NK) cells,3842 macrophages,4347 dendritic cells (DCs),48 and neutrophils.49 50 For example, one investigation demonstrated that synthetic Siglec-7 ligands on cancer cells effectively inhibited NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity.51 Another study, analyzing patient-derived samples, revealed that blocking Siglec-7 and Siglec-9 with specific antibodies enhanced cancer cell killing by primary NK cells and improved immune responses in an immune-deficient mouse model.42 Beyond NK cells, early research also identified the involvement of myeloid cells, including neutrophils and macrophages.50 Siglec-9 and its murine homolog, Siglec-E, were found to mediate immune suppression in the myeloid compartment. Later, Siglec-10 was recognized as a key receptor on tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), interacting with sialylated CD24 to deliver a “don’t eat me” signal, akin to the SIRP1α-CD47 axis.43 Further studies reinforced the role of sialic acid-Siglec interactions in myeloid-driven immune suppression within the TME.47 52 53 In addition, Siglec-7 and Siglec-9 were shown to be elevated on subsets of intratumoral T cells, suggesting their potential role in modulating T cell activity within the TME.35 36

For some Siglecs, there have been great strides made in understanding the precise glycan structures that serve as their ligands; for example, Siglec-7 expressed on NK cells and macrophages has specificity for mucin-type O-glycans,41 54 with the numerous recent studies drilling down on the exact structure involving two or three sialic acids38 55 56 and an additional preference for sulfation.5759 For other Siglecs, such as Siglec-15 on macrophages, the exact glycan structure(s) recognized on cancer cells is not yet as well defined.60 In any case, with mounting evidence of a Siglec-sialoglycan axis serving as a glycoimmune checkpoint that is prominent in cancer, there has been a big push to develop approaches that block this checkpoint, which is explored in more detail below.

### Galectins in cancer immunity

Galectins are GBPs that bind mainly β-galactoside-containing glycoconjugates.61 62 Galectins were described early on as immune-modulatory GBPs.63 Galectins are categorized into three structural groups based on their architecture. The first group, known as prototype galectins (including galectin-1, galectin-2, galectin-5 to galectin-7, galectin-10 to galectin-11, galectin-13 to galectin-14, and galectin-15), has a single carbohydrate recognition domain (CRD) capable of dimerization, allowing them to cross-link with surface molecules on cells. The second group, tandem-repeat galectins (such as galectin-4, galectin-6, galectin-8 to galectin-9, and galectin-12), consists of two CRDs linked by a flexible peptide chain, enabling simultaneous interaction with different glycan structures. Lastly, galectin-3 belongs to the chimera-type group, characterized by a single CRD attached to regions rich in proline and glycine residues. Out of the fourteen galectins identified, humans express twelve, while galectin-5 and galectin-6 are found only in murine tissues.64 65 Galectin-15 is expressed in bovid species but is absent in humans and rodents. In contrast, humans possess galectin-16. Notably, galectins-10, galectins-12, galectins-13 to galectins-14, and galectins-16 exhibit CRDs with mutations that impair their ability to bind galactose. Galectins are expressed intracellularly but can also be secreted by a pathway that does not involve the classical way via the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus.66 67 Galectins have multiple biological functions and influence various processes in cancer progression, including migration, angiogenesis, and anticancer immune functions.61 65 68 69

Galectins have a prominent role in modulating immune cells both by mediating extracellular clustering of receptor complexes and intracellular influencing of signaling and apoptosis.70 Thereby, the innate and adaptive immunity can be modulated by galectin-mediated interactions. For example, T cell signaling is influenced by galectin-3 via preventing T cell receptor (TCR) clustering by binding to branched N-glycans.71 Lack of branched N-glycans by genetic loss of the branching enzyme β1,6-N-acetylglucosaminyltransferase V (MGAT5) led to aggravation of different autoimmune diseases. Galectin-3 binds directly to the glycosylated TCR and thereby increases the T cell activation threshold.72 Galectins have also been shown to influence B cell biology.7375 In cancer, galectins have an influence on T cell activity. For example, galectin-1 can induce regulatory T cell formation in the context of cancer. Apoptosis of effector T helper 1 (TH1), TH17, and CD8+ T cells is regulated by galectin-1 and thereby influencing antitumor immunity.76 77 Galectin-1 was also shown to induce tolerogenic DCs and immune-suppressive macrophages, which can support immune escape and tumor growth.78 79 Galectin-1 further induces programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) and galectin-9 expression in the tumor-associated endothelium.80 Blockade of galectin-1, therefore, increases cytotoxic T cell infiltration. In addition, galectin-9 can induce an immune-suppressive microenvironment by mediating the degradation of STING in cancer cells.81 Galectin-9 interacts directly with T cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain-containing protein 3 (TIM-3), and it was shown that targeting galectin-9 can induce increased numbers of intratumoral cytotoxic T cells.82 83

### C-type lectins in cancer immunity

The immune-regulatory hubs that are established in the TME are complex niches composed of cancer cells, stromal cells, and immune cells where tumor-associated glycans and the correspondent GBPs exert powerful regulatory properties that contribute to cancer immune-editing, defining the nature and the fate of the antitumor immune response. Besides Siglecs and galectins, the C-type lectins are also relevant GBPs in the TME, as they carry one or more CRDs. C-type lectin receptors (CLRs) are membrane-bound proteins that are mainly expressed by antigen-presenting cells such as macrophages and DCs, and some have also been identified on NK cells or endothelial cells.8486

C-type lectins expressed by DCs such as DC-SIGN (DC-specific ICAM-3-grabbing non-integrin 1, also known as CD209), mannose receptor (CD206), macrophage galactose-specific lectin (MGL, also known as CD301), and langerin (on Langerhans cells, CD207) are well characterized for their specificity for high-mannose N-glycans, fucosylated glycans (such as Lewis a,b,x,y), GalNAc or GlcNAc residues, having important functions in tumor immunity.13

The expression of truncated O-glycan structures, such as the Tn antigen (a single residue of GalNAc linked to the serine or threonine of a tumor protein) as well as the Lewis antigens (CD15s) is highly upregulated in malignant cells shaping the creation of immunosuppressive glycan-mediated niches, being widely used as diagnostic and prognostic markers.87 The carbohydrate Lewis antigens, enriched in fucosylated structures, attached to the carcinoembryonic antigen, are commonly detected on colon cancer cells, binding to the C-type lectin DC-SIGN, expressed by macrophages and immature DCs resulting in upregulation of anti-inflammatory cytokines and imposing a suppressive innate immune response.88 89 In line with this, the overexpression of complex branched N-glycans by cancer cells was shown to create a dense glycan shield at the cell surface that precludes proper immunosurveillance, by hampering recognition by DC-SIGN and contributing to immunosuppressive networks through decreased interferon-γ production.8 Concerning O-linked glycans, the expression of Tn epitopes on MUC1 is also linked to poor prognosis in various cancers. Tumor cells displaying Tn glycans expose terminal N-acetylgalactosamine on their surface, which is recognized by MGL on macrophages. This interaction triggers an immune-suppressive response, marked by increased interleukin-10 production and effector T cell apoptosis.90 In line with this, BRAF mutation in patients with stage III colon cancer was shown to induce MGL ligand expression, supporting the immune evasive role of MGL ligands in metastatic disease and poor survival of patients with cancer.91 Furthermore, the C-type lectin dendritic cell immunoreceptor (DCIR), expressed by myeloid cells such as in macrophages, was recently described to have a role in antitumor immunity. Deficiency of DCIR1, the murine homologue of human DCIR, was associated with cancer progression driven by impaired activation of tumor-infiltrating macrophages and T cells along with increased tumor growth.92

### Glycans as targeted antigens

Altered glycan structures on cancer cells and in the TME offer an opportunity to use this cancer-specific glycosignature as potential targets for cancer therapies (figure 1). These so-called tumor-associated carbohydrates (TACA) can be targeted with antibodies or used for potential vaccination strategies.93 For example, the truncated O-glycan sialyl-Thomsen nouveau antigen (sTn) can serve as a potential antigen for antibodies.94 Several monoclonal antibodies have been developed to target sTn, including a recently described specific one.94 95 This antibody is currently being tested in the treatment of ovarian cancer in an antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) format (SGN-STNV) after a phase 1 trial was finished (NCT04665921). Other TACA of interest with clinical success are glycolipids, including the tumor-associated disialoganglioside GD2.96 The antibody dinutuximab has been tested in patients with high-risk neuroblastoma and approved by the Food and Drug Administration in 2015.96 97 Recent applications also test GD2 targeting as an ADC in patients with neuroblastoma.98 Another approach is the use of the single-chain variable fragment (scFv) of the antibody for the generation of CAR-expressing T cells.99 A recent trial tested the intracranial application of GD2-targeting CAR T cells for the treatment of diffuse midline gliomas in children and young adults.100 GD2 that has an O-acetylated sialic acid (OAcGD2) could be even more tumor-specific.101 102 Other potential glycolipid targets are GM3, in particular when it is decorated with the non-human sialic acid N-glycolylneuraminic acid (Neu5Gc)103 and the glycolipid Globo H (Fucα1–2Galβ1–3GalNAcβ1–3Galα1–4Galβ1–4Glc).104 105 The anti-Globo H antibody OBI-888 linked to monomethyl auristatin E (OBI-999) was recently tested in a phase 1 clinical trial.104 The ADC showed a good safety profile with signs of efficacy. Another potential tumor antigen of interest for targeting is Lewis y that was tested clinically as a potential target.106110 In particular, the humanized hu3S193 antibody targeting Lewis y on different solid cancers, including small cell lung cancer, breast cancer, and ovarian cancer, was tested in clinical trials.107 110 111 The efficacy of most studies, however, was rather limited.

### Glycan-engineering of immune effector cells and the tumor microenvironment

In the TME and besides cancer cells, infiltrating T cells are also extensively glycosylated,10 15 being an attractive target for improving the efficacy of immunotherapy. In fact, glycans have been shown to regulate early phases of thymic T cell development with impact in defining effector functions in the periphery and with potential clinical implications.112 113

Glycan structures can be altered in the TME and on cancer cells by delivering small molecules that interfere with monosaccharide or glycan synthesis.114 In addition, glycan-modifying enzymes can be delivered to the TME, for example, with antibodies.53 115 116 The use of sialidase coupled to an antibody targeting a tumor antigen such as HER2 can reduce the sialic acid content within the tumor. A clear effect on inhibiting tumor growth and unleashing an effective antitumor immune response was found in mouse models with HER2-expressing, syngeneic tumor cells treated with bacterial sialidases linked to trastuzumab.53 116 A humanized sialidase is currently being tested in a clinical trial (NCT05259696). Other glycan-modifying enzymes were tested by a similar approach in preclinical mouse models. For example, a bacterial mucinase was used to target heavily O-glycosylated mucins in the TME in a mouse model of subcutaneous tumor growth.115 In another experiment, glycomimetics were used to reduce sialylation in subcutaneous tumors, which led to an increased activation of effector T cells.117 Other small molecules could be used to reduce tumor-associated glycan structures, including inhibitors of fucosylation.118 A broader approach with compounds that inhibit Golgi-localized glycosylation could be used to significantly alter glycan structures in the TME.119 Inhibition of N-glycan branching with an inhibitor also improved antitumor immunity by mediating immune recognition via DC-SIGN.8 Inhibition of N-glycosylation on cancer cells has also been shown to enhance immune effector therapy with anti-CD19 CAR T cells in preclinical models.120 Direct glycan manipulation on ex vivo expanded immune cells prepared for adoptive cell therapies could, therefore, be an interesting option, in particular as genetic modifications of immune cells during GMP production have become a standard option.121 In line with this, T cell genetic glycoengineering of glycogenes involved in branched glycosylation, such as MGAT5, was shown to regulate exhaustion and cytotoxicity of CD8+ T cells in vivo. Deletion of the MGAT5 gene on T cells limits exhaustion, enhancing cytotoxicity and tumor killing, with impact in improving the efficacy of adoptive cellular therapy such as CAR T cells (Pinho Lab, manuscript in preparation). Accordingly, mannose metabolism was shown to reshape T cell differentiation with impact in antitumor immunity.122

### Targeting glycan-binding proteins

Instead of targeting glycans, interactions of glycoconjugates with their cognate receptor can be inhibited. In the case of Siglec receptors, antibodies have been generated to inhibit the interaction with sialoglycans and Siglecs.26 123 124 For example, Siglec-8 targeting antibodies were generated for the treatment of mast cell and eosinophil-mediated diseases.125 This approach had been clinically tested in eosinophilic gastritis and duodenitis. In cancer treatment, Siglec-2 (CD22) and Siglec-3 (CD33) targeting ADCs have been used in hematological diseases but not with the goal to inhibit Siglec-ligand interactions.126 127 Siglec-7 and Siglec-9 blocking antibodies have been tested in preclinical models.128 129 In a mouse model of transgenic human Siglec-7 and Siglec-9 expression, antibody blockade reduced the metastatic burden in lungs of a syngeneic metastasis model.128 Siglec-9 blocking antibodies were successfully tested in ex vivo models of mouse models for glioblastoma.46 130 131 In another work, in vitro neutrophil-mediated cancer cell killing was improved by blocking Siglec-9.129 The use of a Siglec-9 blocking antibody in a humanized mouse model and SKOV3 ovarian cancer cells showed a good effect on the inhibition of tumor progression.131 Others have seen NK activation on Siglec-7 blocking antibody treatment.39 42 51 For example, immunodeficient mice were treated with Siglec-7 expressing DNA vectors in an ovarian cancer xenograft model.39 Siglec receptors have also been targeted with small molecules.132135

Galectins have also been targeted in the context of cancer.65 For example, blocking galectin-1 with an antibody can inhibit angiogenesis.136 Galectin-1 targeting can also enhance cytotoxic T cell activity.137 Other galectins influence cancer immunity, including galectin-3.65 Galectin-9 recognizes the immune checkpoint TIM-3 on T cells and galectin-9 blockade with antibodies has been shown to synergize with agonistic antibodies. Moreover, galectin-binding small molecules can be generated on the basis of β-galactoside derivatives.65 For example, GB1211 is an α-thiogalactoside exhibiting high affinity for galectin-3. A trial combining GB1211 with the PD-L1 blocking atezolizumab is currently ongoing in patient with non-small cell lung cancer (NCT05240131). Derivatives of lactose and galactose were used to test their effect on cancer cells.138 139

The altered glycosylation in the TME is one of the major factors responsible for imposing impaired functions on innate immune response such as those mediated by macrophages and DCs through CLRs recognition of glycan inhibitory signals. In recent years, several studies have emerged highlighting the effects of targeting CLRs on DCs for induction of antitumor immunity.140 The development of DC-SIGN targeting vaccines composed of DC-SIGN ligands, such as glycan-modified dendrimers and liposomes enriched, for example, in Lewis antigens, to target DCs in the TME was shown to facilitate internalization of cancer antigens, favoring antigen cross-presentation and the stimulation of tumor-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses.141 142

Neo-glycoconjugates targeting the mannose receptor were also shown to enhance Toll-like receptor (TLR)-independent cross-presentation and Th1 polarization, further supporting the effects of targeting CLRs in the TME as a vaccination strategy in cancer.143 Moreover, conjugated CD44-Tn-derived glycopeptides, expressed in solid tumors, were recently demonstrated to be immunogenic glycovaccines, inducing both humoral and cellular immunity, including immunological memory.144 Targeting CLRs in the TME via glycan-modified tumor antigens (glycovaccines) is a strategy to improve tumor-specific T cell responses, illustrating the potential effects of cancer glycovaccines single or in combination with immune checkpoint blockade to enhance antitumor immune response and the efficacy of immunotherapy.

### Outlook and conclusion

The use of cancer immunotherapies, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors or the adoptive cellular therapies using tumor-infiltrating T cells or CAR T cells, has revolutionized cancer treatment. However, they are not yet effective in solid tumors, which represent 90% of adult cancers, which constitutes a major unmet need in oncology. One of the major obstacles that hampers the effectiveness of immunotherapies is the immunosuppressive TME. Accumulating evidence has been demonstrating that aberrant cellular glycosylation is one of the earliest events that occur during malignant transformation, having a significant impact in shaping an immunosuppressive TME. The effects of tumor-associated glycans are strongly tied to GBP, expressed or secreted by immune cells, including Siglecs, galectins, and C-type lectins, which establish the immunosuppressive networks of the TME. We also bring to light the power of innovative glycan-engineering strategies of the TME, targeting either cancer cells and/or tumor-infiltrating immune cells, through the use of inhibitory anti-glycan antibodies, glycan-GBP inhibitors or glycosidases coupled to an antibody that targets cancer cells, which holds enormous potential in the development of a new generation of glycan-targeted immunotherapies in cancer.
