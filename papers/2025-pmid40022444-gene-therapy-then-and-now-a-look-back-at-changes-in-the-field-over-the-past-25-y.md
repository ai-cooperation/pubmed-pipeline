## Abstract

Since the inception of Molecular Therapy in 2000, the field of gene therapy has made remarkable progress, evolving from no approved clinical products to 23 clinical gene therapy products today. In this review, we aim to capture the transformative changes in the field by surveying the literature over this period, with a particular focus on advancements in gene delivery vector technology, disease and tissue targeting, and the revolutionary molecular tools that have become central to the field. We also discuss the current challenges facing gene therapy and the need for greater collaboration to ensure its accessibility worldwide.

### Introduction

Molecular Therapy, the official journal of the American Society of Gene and Cell Therapy, published its first issue on January 1, 2000. The 25 years that have transpired since then have witnessed dramatic changes in the scope and direction of all forms of therapeutic delivery of genes and other nucleic acids. This review attempts to broadly summarize the changes that have occurred over that time. It is not possible to enumerate all of the important scientific and technical advances that continually transform this field in one review. However, we have attempted to use library research tools to show major trends in the field and to further illuminate those trends with a few illustrative examples.

The last two decades of the 20th century witnessed two great advances in biomedical research: the harnessing of viruses to accomplish efficient and stable gene expression in host cells and the emergence of techniques such as shotgun cloning and high-throughput sequencing to map the entire human genome.1,2 The human genome sequence provides researchers with the ability to identify genetic cause(s) of human disease, while viral and non-viral vectors provide the vehicles for delivering those genes to host cells.

The first observation that presaged gene therapy was that of Varmus and Bishop in the mid-1970s demonstrating that avian sarcoma viruses caused cancers by shuttling cDNA from certain avian genes, later described as proto-oncogenes, into the genome of cells infected by those viruses.3 Within the following decade, this mechanism was exploited to create gamma retrovirus vectors4,5 for gene transfer. At the same time, adeno-associated virus (AAV) was developed as a mammalian gene transfer vector.6,7 Other viruses capable of long-term latency in host cells, such as adenovirus (AdV) and herpes simplex virus (HSV), were likewise engineered to transfer genes, while non-viral gene transfer methods that were initially developed as research reagents began to show promise for in vivo gene transfer (Figure 1A). By 2000, such tools had been introduced into human trials. The following 25 years have witnessed sweeping changes in the field as well as the emergence of 23 clinical gene therapy products that have received market approval by national or continental regulatory agencies.8 While scientists in future decades may view these efforts as relatively primitive, they remain illuminating for those of us in the field today, as we struggle to overcome human disease in an ever-changing world.Figure 1Publication trends of various vector types(A) The trends in viral and non-viral gene therapy research in the biomedical literature. ### Vectors for gene therapy, the evolving landscape

Gene delivery vector technology has been a cornerstone in the advancement of gene therapy. By 2000, non-viral delivery methods were extensively tested, but generally showed very low gene transfer efficiency, especially for in vivo applications. In contrast, several key viral vector platforms and their production methods had been established after years of development and optimization from the late 1980s and 1990s, including the ones derived from gamma retrovirus, lentivirus, AdV, and AAV. These viral vectors were tested in several early clinical trials targeting cancer and genetic diseases, with some showing initial signs of safe gene transfer. However, results from those trials demonstrated limited clinical efficacy largely due to inefficient gene delivery. The tragic death of Jesse Gelsinger in 1999 due to an adverse reaction to an AdV vector also underscored the risks associated with gene delivery vector.9,10,11 The concerns over efficacy and safety of gene delivery to humans raised after these initial trials was initially viewed as a setback for the gene therapy field, but set the stage for further advancements in vector development for the next 25 years.

### Gamma retrovirus vectors

Gamma retroviruses, such as the murine leukemia virus (MLV), are positive-sense, single-stranded RNA (+ssRNA) viruses with each a 100-nm diameter virion containing two identical copies of an 8 kb of +ssRNA genome complexed with viral proteins in a nucleocapsid, which itself is surrounded by a lipid envelope containing integral envelope glycoproteins. The genome structure includes two long terminal repeats (LTRs) that have promoter elements and are required in cis for packaging and replication of viral RNA as well as viral genes gag-pol and env. Cell entry of the virus is mediated by envelope protein interactions with cell surface receptors, after which the RNA viral genome undergoes reverse transcription by the pol-gene-encoded reverse transcriptase, generating a cDNA copy of the genome, which integrates into the host genome. This feature allows for stable, long-term expression of the transgene. Gamma retrovirus vectors based on Moloney MLV were developed in the 1980s as described above, and became one of the major tools for gene transfer by 2000 (Figure 1B). Their application was limited to dividing cells because gamma retroviruses must access the host genome during mitosis after nuclear membrane breakdown. Gamma retrovirus vectors were used in milestone clinical trials, including the treatment of severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID), with the first human trial being treatment of adenosine deaminse-SCID and the first clinically efficacious work being done to treat X-linked SCID.12,13,14 However, despite the exciting clinical efficacy reported in 2000, the occurrence of leukemia in five treated patients reported in 2003 re-ignited concerns about the risks of insertional oncogenesis characteristic of these viruses,15,16,17 as they naturally integrate into the host cell genome as a vital portion of their life cycle.18 While gamma retrovirus vectors were foundational in the early days of gene therapy, by 2024 their use had become less common, as they have largely been replaced in clinical applications by lentiviral vectors, which have shown a better safety profile due to a different integration pattern (Figure 1B).

### Lentivirus vectors

Lentiviruses are a distinct type of retroviruses. Lentiviral vectors, most based on HIV-1, have two copies of ssRNA, and integrate proviral DNA into the host genome. Lentiviral vectors were developed in late 1990s for their ability to transduce both dividing and non-dividing cells.19 Within only a few years, packaging systems were refined to reduce the risk of generating replication-competent lentivirus.20,21 The generation of self-inactivating (SIN) LTRs that lack promoter activity after integration were developed to minimize the risk of oncogene activation near the integration sites.22 Since 2000, SIN lentiviral vectors have increasingly been favored over retrovirus vectors (Figure 1) in part due to their better efficiency in transducing non-dividing cells, such as hematopoietic stem cells (discussed below), and a distinct integration pattern.23 By 2024, lentivirus vectors had largely replaced gamma retrovirus vectors for a variety of ex vivo gene therapy development.

### AdV vectors

Unlike retroviruses, the many different serotypes of AdVs are non-enveloped, double-stranded DNA viruses that generally produce transient, lytic infections in host cells, which is an important limitation of this virus. Their 36-kb genomes replicate as extrachromosomal DNA elements (episomes), although latent infections also develop in some cells. The initial AdV vectors based on group C adenoviruses (AdV5 or AdV2), in which the AdV early genes E1a and E1b were deleted, were developed around 1990 and expressed several viral genes along with the transgene, causing severe immune responses following in vivo administration.24,25 The first human use of AdV vectors was first reported in 1993 in cystic fibrosis (CF) patients and published the following year.26 Later refinement by 1998 removed additional early genes or all viral genes, leading to an improved safety profile.27,28 However, even the gutless AdV vectors lacking all viral genes (also known as high-capacity AdVs [HC-AdVs]) were shown to cause both innate and adaptive immune responses to the capsid. In addition, production of HC-AdVs is helper dependent, which leads to contamination of wild-type AdV in preparations of therapeutic vectors. Nevertheless, by 2000, AdV was the most widely studied gene delivery vector, especially for in vivo applications (Figure 1B). Conditionally replicating AdVs were also extensively studied as oncolytic viruses due to their preferential replication in tumor cells, and some strategies employed transgenes to enhance antitumor functions, such as Gendicine, an oncolytic AdV carrying the P53 gene for head and neck cancer that was approved in China as the first commercial gene therapy product.

As AAV vectors emerged as a safer alternative for in vivo gene delivery (discussed below), AdV vectors have been used less often. By 2024, AdV vectors had largely been replaced by AAV vectors in developing therapies to treat monogenic diseases where long-term gene transfer is desired (Figure 1B), although they remain useful for vaccine development, such as the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) vaccines, oncolytic therapies, and local gene transfer, when much lower doses are required to mitigate immune responses. Ad vectors have also undergone extensive preclinical and clinical evaluation as therapies for impaired vascularization and heart failure when transient expression of growth factors is needed to promote angiogenesis.29,30,31 Further studies are necessary to confirm the clinical efficacy observed in early-stage human trials.32,33,34

### AAV vectors

AAV has a small, single-stranded DNA viral genome of approximately 4.7 kb. In non-dividing cells, the viral DNA can persist in a variety of circular and linear episomal forms in the nucleus along with a smaller percentage of integrated AAV genomes. AAV is classified as a Dependovirus, which is a Parvovirus that is naturally defective for replication and requires co-infection with a helper virus, such as an AdV, to enter into the replicative phase of its life cycle. Importantly, infection of cells with AAV in the absence of helper virus leads to long-term latency. Although AAV was first discovered in 1960s, it was first reported as a gene delivery vector in 1984, as described above.6 The first in vivo use in animals reported was direct endobronchial delivery in rabbits.35 The first use for human gene delivery was in CF patients in 1995, which was first published the following year.36,37 All of this initial work was based on the AAV2 serotype. The late 1990s to early 2000s saw major breakthroughs for AAV vector development, including long-term transgene expression in rabbits, non-human primates, mouse, and dog models35,38,39,40; design of self-complementary AAV to improve transduction efficiency41,42; and the discovery of novel AAV capsid serotypes to target a wider range of tissues.43,44 These advancements spurred extensive testing of AAV vectors for in vivo gene delivery and gene therapy at preclinical and clinical stages in the ensuing years. A large number of those studies focused on characterizing the tissue tropism of various AAV serotype vectors and delivering protein-coding transgenes to correct monogenic deficiencies.

By 2024, AAV vectors had become the preferred delivery vehicle for in vivo gene therapy (Figure 1B) and are used to target various tissues and deliver a broad spectrum of therapeutic cargos, including regulatory RNAs and gene-editing tools (discussed below). Currently, all AAV gene therapy products that have received regulatory approval are based on naturally occurring capsids (Table 1). However, these capsids are not ideal for use as human gene therapy vectors.45 For example, high vector doses are required to achieve therapeutic efficacy in certain cases, which risks toxicities and mortality.46 As a result, AAV capsid development continues to be an important research topic, with an increasing focus on engineering capsids for improved cross-species or human-specific tropism for translational gene therapy applications. These engineered capsids hold great promise to deliver safer and more efficacious gene therapy in the near future, and many are currently in clinical trials. ### HSV vectors

HSVs, such as HSV type 1 (HSV-1), have a linear double-stranded DNA viral genome that takes an episomal, circular configuration in the host nucleus. HSV-1 can establish either a lytic or a latent infection depending on the host cell type. HSV-1 vectors were developed as a non-replicative gene delivery vehicle for neurons due to HSV-1’s natural neuronal tropism and tendency to establish latency in neurons.50,51,52,53,54 In other settings, conditionally replicating HSV-1 or recombinant HSV-1 vectors have been developed for oncolytic therapies to treat recalcitrant cancers, including one approval in Japan for glioblastoma multiforme. While oncolytic HSVs were extensively studied and gained regulatory approval in 2015, neurotropic HSV gene therapy development was more limited to pain management.55,56 In general, AAV vectors remain to be the preferred in vivo delivery platform for neurological diseases, partially explaining the decreased use of HSV vectors in the past 25 years (Figure 1B). Recently, a non-replicative HSV gene therapy known as beremagene geperpavec (B-VEC) gained U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval to treat dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa, a rare genetic blistering skin disease caused by mutations in COL7A1.57 B-VEC is designed for repeated topical use due to its low immunogenicity, a distinctive feature of HSV vectors. Developing HSV gene therapy for a broader range of clinical applications would benefit from innovative approaches to establish stable, latent-like infection in non-neuronal cell types.

### Non-viral delivery vectors

Numerous non-viral carriers have been developed to shuttle nucleic acids into cells. They generally have a better safety profile than viral vectors, including lower risks of immune responses and insertional mutagenesis, and a more streamlined manufacturing capability. However, the efficiency of non-viral delivery by 2000 was still lower compared to viral methods. The next 25 years witnessed important advancements in non-viral gene therapy delivery. In 2024, lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) are quickly becoming the most widely used non-viral delivery method (Figure 1C). This is mostly driven by their applications in delivering RNA molecules, including small interfering RNA (siRNA), mRNA, and guide RNA for CRISPR-based gene editing. The success of LNP-mRNA COVID vaccines has also significantly advanced LNP technology. The advantages of using non-viral methods to deliver gene-editing tools are increasingly recognized due to their transient nature and large cargo capacity. Targeting extrahepatic tissues remains a major challenge despite recent progress.

### Biological innovations change the nature of the cargo in the 21st century

Early gene therapies by 2000 typically involved delivering a protein-coding gene to correct genetic deficiencies in recessive disorders or to endow cells with better antitumor functions. Although such gene replacement and gene addition approaches still account for most gene therapy applications at present, emerging biological innovations have opened more avenues to manipulate endogenous genes and their expression. Two notable examples are RNA interference (RNAi) for gene silencing and CRISPR for gene editing, both of which are Nobel Prize-winning discoveries and have become major gene therapy modalities by 2024. Their rapid advancements toward use in the clinic have benefited from the existing delivery methods used for traditional gene therapy. However, these new cargos with unique features, such as small non-coding RNAs and large gene editors, bring delivery challenges to fully unleash their therapeutic potential.

### RNAi

RNAi is a cellular process that involves small, double-stranded RNA, known as siRNA, to degrade complementary mRNA. siRNAs are typically 20–25 base pairs long and can be delivered to cells as exogenous RNA molecules to exert a silencing effect on endogenous gene expression. Shortly after its discovery in C. elegans in 1998,58 the therapeutic potential of RNAi was quickly recognized and studied in mammalian cells by 2000. Early development of therapeutic siRNA drugs faced several challenges including the instability of siRNA caused by enzymatic degradation, inefficient delivery of siRNA across cell membrane due to the large size and negative charge, and innate immune responses triggered by double-stranded RNA. These issues were effectively overcome, at least for targeting hepatocytes of the liver by chemical RNA modifications,59 conjugation with the GalNAc targeting ligand,60 and augmenting LNP delivery technologies,61 ultimately leading to six FDA-approved siRNA therapies since 2018. By 2024, innovative chemistry has expanded siRNA therapy development to target other tissue types such as the lung and brain.62,63

In the 2000s, short hairpin RNA (shRNA) technology was developed to overcome the transient nature of exogenous siRNA molecules.64,65,66 shRNA can be continuously expressed from delivered DNA, such as plasmids or viral vectors, and has a stem-loop structure that is processed by cellular machinery into siRNA. shRNA makes it possible to generate sustained RNAi-based gene silencing effects as long as the vector maintains durable gene expression. However, AAV vector-delivered shRNA has been found to cause fatality in mice because its cellular processing overlaps with and oversaturates the biogenesis pathway of microRNAs (miRNAs). miRNA is another Nobel Prize-winning biological discovery,67,68 and understanding its mechanism has facilitated the optimization of vectored shRNA designs. For example, embedding shRNA into a natural miRNA backbone generates an artificial miRNA (amiRNA) that has an improved safety profile due to a controlled expression level, greater accuracy of processing, and better target specificity.69,70,71 By 2024, AAV vectors delivering shRNA or amiRNA have reached clinical testing, especially in target organs where non-viral delivery of siRNA remains a challenge.72

### Gene editing

Zinc finger nucleases and meganucleases were the major targeted gene-editing tools in early 2000s. Both rely on protein-DNA interactions for sequence specificity and make double-strand breaks in DNA, then rely on the cellular DNA repair mechanisms to introduce edits near the cut sites. In 2010, transcription activator-like effector nucleases became a more popular customizable gene-editing tool due to their simple, modular repeats architecture. The revolutionary breakthrough in genomic editing came in 2012,73 when studies showed that the CRISPR-Cas9 systems derived from bacteria could be engineered for precise gene editing in mammalian cells. Compared with previous tools relying on protein-based DNA recognition, the RNA-guided CRISPR-Cas9 has higher on-target efficiency, lower off-target effects, and easier programmability, and therefore quickly became the preferred technology for gene editing.

The initial therapeutic applications of CRISPR-based gene editing still relied on double-strand breaks and DNA repair, such as homology-directed repair (HDR) or non-homologous end-joining (NHEJ). NHEJ is very effective in eliminating the function of targeted DNA sequences, such as with exagamglogene autotemceld (Casgevy), which inactivates the erythroid specific enhancer element of BCL11A, the gene that transcriptionally inactivates fetal hemoglobin. This treatment allows for high expression levels of fetal hemoglobin, overcoming mutations in beta globin responsible for sickle cell disease and beta thalassemia.74 In contrast, CRISPR approaches that rely on HDR are more limited in utility. For example, the HDR pathway is not active in non-dividing cells. To overcome these drawbacks, base editing and prime editing were invented by David Liu and colleagues.75,76 These gene editors operate somewhat independently from cellular DNA repair pathways and can achieve precise and customizable genomic edits. By 2024, various CRISPR-based gene-editing strategies have been tested in preclinical and clinical stages, with the one above-mentioned CRISPR-modified cell therapy for sickle cell disease and thalassemia approved by the FDA.

Compared with traditional gene therapy, CRISPR-based gene editors introduce additional considerations in terms of delivery. While non-viral gene editing, particularly ex vivo strategies as with Casgevy, is quite feasible, applications of in vivo gene editing present additional challenges. The genes encoding the Cas9 nucleases are relatively large and long-lasting therapeutic effects only require their transient action in host cells; however, the prolonged presence of gene editors has been shown to increase off-target effects. These features make non-viral methods particularly suitable for CRISPR delivery, although viral delivery strategies have been developed, such as using AAV vectors to deliver a DNA template for HDR.77 Accompanying the rapid advancements in gene-editing technologies is the swift progress in assessing off-target gene-editing events, including in silico, biochemical, and cellular-based assays. These genome-wide analyses help to reduce bias in identifying potential off-target sites and are recommended by the FDA as an integral component of safety assessments for gene-editing therapeutics.78

### Disease and tissue targeting, clinical translation, and commercialization

The diseases targeted by gene therapy research over the past quarter century have been influenced by two major factors: the ability to efficiently target certain cell/tissue types and the extent of the unmet medical need presented by each disease. Perhaps the best example of choosing disease targets based on a vector’s performance is targeting of genetic retinal diseases, such as inherited retinal dystrophy due to RPE-65 deficiency, which is neither life threatening nor highly prevalent.79 The first FDA-approved AAV vector, voretigene neparvovec-rzyl (Luxturna), is an AAV2 vector administered by subretinal (SR) injection that advanced more rapidly than most therapies because SR delivery results in highly efficient transduction of retinal pigment epithelial cells, even when delivered using the earliest identified AAV serotype, AAV2, and with relatively small amounts of vector (1.5E+11 vg/eye). By comparison, this dose is approximately a 10,000-fold lower vg dose per patient than a typical patient dose of the recently FDA-approved therapy for Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), delandistrogene moxeparvovec-rokl (Elevidys). For example, while the total dose of Luxturna for a 30-kg, 10-year-old child would be 3.0E+11 vg, the total patient dose of Elevidys for the same patient would be 3.99E+15 vg, a 13,300-fold higher total vg dose.80 This at least partially explains why FDA approval occurred in 2017 for Luxturna, while provisional approval of Elevidys occurred 6 years later in 2023. While many factors, such as the funding raised by disease foundations, National Institutes of Health (NIH) institutes, and other agencies have also influenced disease choice over time, the fundamental biological limitations of vector tropism, manufacturing limitations, and the safety and tolerability of very high systemic doses of AAV vectors create the “boundary conditions” to allow for full clinical approvals. ### Cancer

Throughout the history of gene therapy, there have been numerous efforts to use gene therapy technology to meet the tremendous unmet need of cancer morbidity and mortality. In 2000, the cancer death rate in the United States was 196.5 deaths per 100,000 population and by 2022 it had only receded modestly to 182.5, which amounts to more than 600,000 deaths per year in the United States.81 The other factor spurring application of gene and cell therapy for cancer has been the risk-benefit proposition. As long as gene and cell therapy carries a substantial risk, its application to a clinical problem will be facilitated by the potential benefits of treating diseases that otherwise have a higher risk of death.

It is not surprising, therefore, that the first human use of gene transfer technology was by Dr. Steven Rosenberg at the National Cancer Institute (NCI) in 1989. This was a cell-marking protocol in which tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes isolated from patients with metastatic malignant melanoma were marked with a neo gene using a gamma retrovirus vector, expanded, and then reinfused as a potential immunotherapy.82 It is likewise not surprising that cancer was both the most common disease application associated with gene therapy publications in the literature in 2000 and remains so in recent years (Figure 2).

### Primary immune deficiencies

As with the use of genetically modified T cells for cancer immunotherapy, primary immune deficiency diseases have been a favored target of genetic therapies because the salient cell types, lymphocytes and hematopoietic stem cells, are cells that are readily transduced ex vivo with gamma retroviral or lentiviral vectors. Shortly after the first gene transfer trial at NCI mentioned above, the first therapeutic gene therapy for a primary immunodeficiency disorder was undertaken in 1990, also on the NIH campus.12 That first trial was undertaken in patients with genetic deficiency of adenosine deaminase (ADA), which was treated with an expanded autologous T cell product transduced with a gamma retroviral vector expressing ADA, but other protocols soon followed that used transduction of hematopoietic stem cells.14 It was noted that very low percentages of corrected cells were transduced in the initial trials.93

Over the last 25 years, primary immune deficiency gene therapy has expanded to include clinical development of therapies for several other gene defects beyond ADA deficiency, most notably for X-linked SCID, chronic granulomatous disease, and Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome.94 Protocols targeting this class of disorders has also encountered important obstacles, most notably vector-induced leukemogenesis due to insertional activation of the LMO2 protooncogene.17 However, the clinical efficacy demonstrated in these patients has been deemed sufficient to justify ongoing clinical trials with modified SIN LTRs, which aim to reduce the risks of insertional mutagensis.95,96 Since 2000, the applications of gene therapy for primary immune deficiencies have proceeded steadily and expanded. A retroviral-transduced CD34+ hematopoietic stem cell therapy for ADA (Strimvelis) received commercial approval in the European Union in 2016. Unfortunately, this clinically effective therapy was removed from the market in 2023 due to market failure.97 The limitations of the commercialization of human gene therapy imposed by small patient population size rather than by biological efficacy will be discussed further below.

### Cardiovascular diseases

As with cancer gene therapy, cardiovascular diseases have been common targets for gene therapy due to the high prevalence of these disorders. Also like cancer, cardiovascular diseases are multigenic and multifactorial, causing some uncertainty as to whether the most common diseases can be treated with gene modification targeting a single gene. This is at least part of the reason why the number of publications per year regarding gene therapy for these disorders has decreased since 2000 rather than increasing. Genetic cardiomyopathies are simpler conceptually, relying generally on correction of the primary gene defect both within the heart and systemically, as with DMD, Danon disease, or Pompe disease.111,112 Arrhythmogenic conditions addressed by gene therapy may include those induced by single gene defects or by acquired states such as in patients shortly after surgical repair of aortic valve disease.107,113 Finally, one of the most successful gene therapies for cardiovascular disease as of 2025 is the siRNA specific for PCSK9.110

The more recent article describes a clinical stage (now FDA-approved) therapy using a subcutaneously injected siRNA technology that had not yet been developed in 2000 (Figure 3B). ### Eye diseases

The most dramatic increase in publications in gene therapy has been in the field of eye diseases (Figure 2). This has clearly been fueled by the remarkably high efficiency of gene transfer accomplished with SR injection of AAV vectors. In 2000, the ability of AAV to accomplish highly efficient gene transfer in the retina after SR injection had already been established by the Hauswirth laboratory.114 However, the FDA approval of Luxturna (voretigene neparvovec-rzyl) in 2017 drew in dramatically more interest in developing ocular gene therapies from translational researchers across academia and industry.

### Metabolic disorders

Metabolic disorders, or inborn errors of metabolism, represent some of the best-described single-gene disorders, and so have been prominent targets for gene therapy. As most of these diseases require gene transfer primarily to the liver, the feasibility of viral vector-mediated transduction of the liver had been established to a reasonable extent.115 In 2000, the potential safety concerns with systemic AdV vector gene therapy that emerged after Jesse Gelsinger’s death remained central to considerations of gene therapy for metabolic disorders. As the relative safety of intravenous delivery of AAV vectors became evident, interest in targeting the liver to treat patients with metabolic disorders has grown substantially. While hemophilias A and B are not, strictly speaking, metabolic disorders, the clinical efficacy and FDA approvals of gene therapies targeting the liver to treat them have drawn in a great degree of interest and impact.116,117

### CNS diseases

The status of AAV gene therapy for CNS disorders somewhat parallels that with metabolic disorders, in that the preclinical proof-of-concept studies were published in 2000 with AAV vectors, as well as HSV vectors, Ad vectors (including helper-dependent Ad), and lentiviral vectors.118 However, critical breakthroughs enabling true clinical translation have been made since 2000, most notably the use of self-complementary AAV9 in newborns to cross the blood-brain barrier sufficiently to treat spinal muscular atrophy type 1,119 which ultimately led to pivotal clinical studies120,121 (Figure 4) and FDA approval of Zolgensma. ### Muscle diseases

The extensive disease advocacy community has supported research into gene therapy for primary muscle disease, including muscular dystrophies, from the earliest days of gene therapy. The upward trends in total publications on gene therapy for muscle diseases observed since 2000 have been accelerated by findings of clinical efficacy and FDA approval of Elevidys for DMD.122 Thus, the trends in this field roughly parallel those seen with retina and metabolic disease gene therapy.

### Lung diseases

As with muscular dystrophies, the very strong advocacy and funding effort in gene therapy by the CF Foundation fueled early enthusiasm for gene therapy for lung diseases. In fact, the first human uses of both AdV and AAV vectors were in patients with CF.26,36,37 The limitations of stable gene transfer to the lung using AdV, AAV, and non-viral vectors combined with the availability of small molecule CFTR modulators to treat CF have led to a decline in the number of publications in this field. One article published in the January 1, 2000, Molecular Therapy issue presents the clinical trial outcomes of a landmark study of non-viral gene therapy for CF.123 The study showed persistence only in the range of 3–10 days after dosing of a plasmid-liposome DNA complex, without evidence of physiologic correction. Interestingly, many of the pulmonary-related publications in Molecular Therapy in 2024 have dealt with infectious diseases, including COVID-19, taking advantage of transient expression to create either vaccines, other immunotherapies, or modifications in severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 binding.

### Gene therapy in the next 25 years

Over the past 25 years, gene therapy researchers have overcome numerous barriers to alter gene expression in patients’ cells sufficiently to reverse most or all of the clinical manifestations of a number of severe human diseases. Many other diseases seem to be within reach in the coming years. Once a gene therapy product is proven to be safe and effective for a disease affecting a particular organ or tissue, other diseases affecting the same organ or tissue can potentially be addressed with the same vector platform. New variations of AAV capsids and improved targeting technology for LNPs and other non-viral platforms are allowing gene therapies to reach cells and tissues that were previously difficult to transduce. The continually evolving tools of gene editing are becoming ever more efficient, specific, and safe. From all of these perspectives, the next quarter century of gene therapy research holds great promise. However, the current state of gene therapy presents a number of daunting challenges for the coming 25 years. Current gene transfer platforms still have some undesired properties, such as the immunogenicity of AAV and the potential of carcinogenesis from all viral vectors. Safety assessments for gene-editing therapeutics continue to face challenges in evaluating genomic integrity, potential oncogenic risks, and the biological consequences of both off-target and on-target, unintended gene-editing events. Increasing the precision and efficiency of genetic manipulation may well require additional biological breakthroughs, which like RNAi and CRISPR-based gene editing, will only occur with continued investment into basic research and discovery of fundamental cellular pathways and processes.

Surprisingly, one of most difficult issues for gene therapy in the coming years is economic rather than biological. While gene therapy scientists have overcome many of the biological barriers to therapeutic gene transfer, the funding required to develop, then manufacture and distribute gene therapies now represent a substantial barrier as well. This is particularly true for rare and ultrarare diseases, many of which are single-gene disorders.97,124 The success of each gene therapy increases the opportunity to create similar therapies for disorders that are phenotypically similar but require a different gene to be replaced or repaired. Given that there are approximately 7,000 rare diseases, and many of them affect patient populations so small that the market for new medicines is too small to be commercially viable in the currently existing paradigm, new models of clinical development must be pursued in the future. Similarly, the dissemination of gene therapies globally, particularly to low and middle income countries adds to the call for a different model of financial sustainability.125 Two of the diseases targeted by currently available commercial gene therapies, sickle cell disease and beta thalassemia, have a much broader representation in LMICs than in prosperous western countries. Leaders in the field are increasingly recognizing the ethical imperative to provide access to gene therapy technology to all people who can benefit from it, regardless of how rare their disease is or how much wealth they possess. One of the biggest challenges that faces the field at the moment is the development and application of economic platforms which, like the biological platforms of gene therapy, requires innovation and progress by researchers in cooperation with regulatory agencies, patient advocacy organizations, and the public around the world.
