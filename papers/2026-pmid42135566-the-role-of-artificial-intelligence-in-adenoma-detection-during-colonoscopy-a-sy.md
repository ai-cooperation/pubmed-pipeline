## Abstract

**BACKGROUND**: Colonoscopy is the gold standard for colorectal cancer screening; however, it is limited by a substantial adenoma miss rate. We aimed to provide an updated and comprehensive synthesis of evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) on the efficacy of artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted versus conventional colonoscopy in detecting colorectal adenomas.

**METHODS**: A systematic literature search of PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science was conducted up to 6 May 2025. Primary outcomes included the adenoma detection rate (ADR), adenomas per colonoscopy (APC), sessile serrated lesion detection rate (SSLDR), SSLs per colonoscopy (SSLPC), adenoma miss rate (AMR), and SSL miss rate (SSLMR). Pooled risk ratios (RR) and mean differences (MD) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using a random-effects model.

**RESULTS**: A total of 46 RCTs, involving 37,206 participants, were included. AI-assisted colonoscopy significantly increased ADR (RR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.17-1.27, I2 = 60.49%), SSLDR (RR = 1.25, 95% CI: 1.12-1.40, I2 = 11.08%), APC (MD = 0.22, 95% CI: 0.18-0.26, P < 0.001), and SSLPC (MD = 0.02, 95% CI: 0.005-0.04, P = 0.01). AI also substantially reduced the AMR (RR = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.44-0.65, P < 0.001) but not SSLMR (RR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.25-1.66, P = 0.36).

**CONCLUSIONS**: This comprehensive meta-analysis confirms that AI-assisted colonoscopy significantly improves the detection of conventional adenomas and reduces miss rates. While it also improves the SSLDR, its effect on reducing their miss rate remains inconclusive.

### Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most commonly diagnosed cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally, with an estimated 1,926,425 new cases and 904,019 deaths in 2022 [1]. Screening is a cornerstone of CRC prevention, crucial for reducing both mortality and the economic burden of treatment [2]. Colonoscopy is the gold standard screening method, allowing for the direct visualization of the colorectal mucosa to detect and remove precancerous lesions [3]. However, its efficacy is limited by a miss rate of 26% for adenomas (95% confidence interval [CI]: 23–30%) and 27% for serrated polyps (95% CI: 16–40%), as demonstrated by tandem colonoscopy studies [4]. Consequently, various strategies have been developed to improve the adenoma detection rate (ADR) of conventional colonoscopy [5].

Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly being integrated into colonoscopy to mitigate human cognitive errors and enhance ADR. AI-assisted colonoscopy typically employs computer-aided detection (CADe) systems, which are based on machine learning algorithms. These systems are trained on large datasets of endoscopic findings and subsequently analyze the video feed in real time to highlight suspicious lesions [6]. A second AI modality, computer-assisted quality (CAQ), aims to improve the quality of the examination by ensuring adequate mucosal inspection during the withdrawal phase [7].

Numerous studies have evaluated the impact of AI systems on adenoma detection. Previous meta-analyses, synthesizing up to 33 trials, have demonstrated that AI-assisted colonoscopy significantly improves ADR, reduces the adenoma miss rate (AMR), and increases the number of adenomas detected per colonoscopy (APC) [8, 9]. Nonetheless, the rapid evolution of this field necessitates an updated synthesis. To incorporate recent evidence and achieve greater statistical power through a more comprehensive search—including databases such as Scopus [10], we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis on the role of AI in adenoma detection during colonoscopy by including randomized controlled trials (RCTs).

### Protocol

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [11] and was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration no. CRD420251173212. The protocol of this study was approved by the Ethical Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (IR.SUMS.REC.1404.481).

### Search strategy and screening

A systematic literature search was performed in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science from inception until 6 May 2025. The complete search strategy for PubMed is available in Supplementary Box 1. To ensure the identification of all eligible studies, we also manually screened the reference lists of included articles and recent relevant systematic reviews. We included all peer-reviewed RCTs—with either parallel or tandem designs—that compared AI-assisted colonoscopy (utilizing CADe or CAQ systems) against conventional colonoscopy. Studies were eligible if the indication for colonoscopy was screening, surveillance, or diagnostic evaluation of symptoms. The primary outcome was the ADR. Secondary outcomes included APC, sessile serrated lesion detection rate (SSLDR), SSL per colonoscopy (SSLPC), AMR, and SSL miss rate (SSLMR). We excluded studies with non-RCT designs, those utilizing other AI systems, and those with other control groups or outcome measures.

Following the database search, all identified records were exported to EndNote 21, and removal of duplicated records was conducted. Study screening was performed independently by two reviewers (S.S.Z. and M.B.). The initial phase consisted of a title and abstract review to exclude clearly irrelevant studies. Records that appeared eligible or for which a decision was uncertain based on the title and abstract alone advanced to the second phase for a full-text assessment. Any discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved through consensus or by a third reviewer (A.B.).

### Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by the same two reviewers using a standardized form to collect the following data from each included study: first author, year of publication, country, study design, sample size, sex distribution, mean age, mean body mass index (BMI), AI system name and function, and the outcomes assessed.

For the meta-analysis, data were obtained either through direct extraction from the studies or indirectly estimated by the reviewers using standard formulas for each outcome. The ADR and SSLDR were defined as the proportion of patients with at least one adenoma or SSL, respectively, divided by the total number of colonoscopies performed. The AMR and SSLMR were calculated as the number of adenomas or SSLs detected exclusively during the second pass of tandem colonoscopies, divided by the total number of such lesions identified across both procedures [9]. When necessary, means and standard deviations (SDs) for APC and SSLPC were derived from reported medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) or standard errors (SEs) using established methods [12]. When the SD was not reported, it was imputed on the basis of the average SD from other comparable studies, in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook. In studies reporting two relevant intervention groups compared with a single control group, the sample size of the control group was divided to create two independent pairwise comparisons to avoid double counting [13], or the data from the two intervention groups were combined to form a single group for comparison [14]. For tandem trials, data were exclusively extracted from the initial colonoscopy to mitigate potential carryover effects.

### Risk of bias assessment

Two independent reviewers (S.S.Z. and M.B.) assessed the risk of bias for all included studies using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for Randomized Trials (RoB 2) [15]. The results were visualized using Review Manager (RevMan 5) software.

### Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using STATA (version 17) with a random-effects model owing to the methodological heterogeneity and the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation method. Results are presented as risk ratio (RR) for dichotomous and mean difference (MD) for continuous outcomes, with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Between-study statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using I2 statistics and Cochran’s Q test. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity and to examine differences in effect sizes across different study characteristics. Subgroups containing zero or one study were not reported. A sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the robustness of the pooled results and to determine the influence of any individual study on the overall effect estimate. Publication bias was assessed for outcomes that included more than ten studies, using funnel plot inspection and Egger’s and Begg’s tests. In cases where significant publication bias was indicated, the trim-and-fill method was applied. A two-sided P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

The use of AI during colonoscopy was also associated with a reduced AMR (RR = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.44–0.65, P-value < 0.001), but not with a significant reduction in the SSLMR (RR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.25–1.66, P-value = 0.36), with substantial heterogeneity (Supplementary Figs. S3 and S4). Study location, number of centers, and AI system were identified as sources of heterogeneity in these analyses (Table 2).

AI-assisted colonoscopy also significantly increased the mean number of APC (MD = 0.22, 95% CI: 0.18–0.26, P-value < 0.001, I2 = 45.95%) and SSLPC (MD = 0.02, 95% CI: 0.005–0.04, P-value = 0.01, I2 = 10.27%, Supplementary Figs. S5, S6). Study design, location, and AI system were sources of heterogeneity in the APC analysis. The increase in APC was significantly greater with the ENDO-AID system compared with others (P-value for subgroup difference = 0.03). No other significant differences in effect sizes were observed between subgroups (Table 2).

Significant publication bias was detected in the analyses of ADR, APC, and SSLPC (Supplementary Fig. S7). The trim-and-fill analysis imputed 11, 14, and 1 study for these analyses, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S8). This sensitivity analysis suggested that unpublished data would be unlikely to substantially alter the overall observed effects (ADR: RR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.11–1.22; APC: MD = 0.15, 95% CI: 0.13–0.17; SSLPC: MD = 0.02, 95% CI: 0.004–0.04). Furthermore, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses confirmed that no single study exerted a significant influence on the pooled effect estimates.

### Discussion

This meta-analysis of RCTs demonstrates that AI-assisted colonoscopy significantly enhances key performance metrics compared with conventional colonoscopy. Specifically, AI was associated with a 22% increase in the ADR, a 25% increase in the SSLDR, a mean increase of 0.22 in the APC, and a mean increase of 0.02 in the SSLPC. Furthermore, AI assistance resulted in a substantial 47% reduction in the AMR. Although these effect sizes are considered modest, as indicated by the proximity of the point estimates to the reference line [58], they may still hold important clinical significance, reinforcing AI as a promising technology in CRC prevention. Our findings are consistent with those of previous studies in this field [8, 9, 59]. However, by searching more comprehensive databases and including 46 studies encompassing 37,206 participants—substantially larger than in earlier meta-analyses—this study provides a timely, comprehensive, and methodologically robust update to the current evidence. This enhances the precision of the pooled estimates and offers one of the most comprehensive updates to date regarding the clinical applicability of AI-assisted colonoscopy.

Our analysis demonstrated a significant increase in the SSLDR, a finding that surpasses the 10% improvement reported in a prior meta-analysis [8]. However, we found that the SSLMR did not differ significantly between AI-assisted and conventional colonoscopy. This finding suggests that while AI may be effective in identifying more apparent SSLs, its ability to detect all, particularly subtle, SSLs during a single examination remains limited. This discrepancy may be attributed to the limited number of studies included in the analysis or the relative scarcity of SSLs in the datasets used to train AI models. Consequently, SSL detection remains challenging not only for human endoscopists but also for current AI-based systems [60]. These results highlight the need to train AI systems on larger, dedicated SSL datasets to enhance their effectiveness in achieving comprehensive detection and removal of these important precursors of CRC.

In the present study, the level of endoscopist expertise was identified as a potential source of heterogeneity. However, no statistically significant difference was observed between expert and non-expert endoscopists regarding the effect of AI on ADR. This finding is consistent with the results of a previous meta-analysis [8], suggesting that both groups may benefit from AI-assisted colonoscopy. Nevertheless, this benefit may differ in real-world practice, where less experienced endoscopists could derive greater advantage from AI support, as it may compensate for developing pattern-recognition skills. The definition of “expert endoscopist” varied across studies—most commonly defined as those with a baseline ADR of ≥ 20% and experience of more than 1000 lifetime colonoscopies—highlighting the need for future studies employing standardized criteria to draw more definitive conclusions on this issue.

A key finding from our subgroup analysis was that the beneficial effect of AI on ADR was significantly more pronounced with the use of CAQ, either alone or in combination with CADe, compared with CADe systems alone. This suggests that an AI-driven approach that integrates lesion recognition with the optimization of examination quality may yield a greater incremental benefit for adenoma detection. However, the current body of literature directly comparing these AI modalities remains limited, highlighting an important area for future research.

Several technical and human factors warrant consideration during the implementation of AI-assisted colonoscopy. One important phenomenon is alert fatigue, whereby endoscopists may become desensitized to frequent AI-generated alarms, particularly when a significant proportion are false positives. This desensitization could potentially diminish operator responsiveness and the overall effectiveness of the system over time [61]. Furthermore, the learning curve associated with AI-assisted colonoscopy—referring to the adaptation period required for endoscopists to effectively integrate AI prompts into their behavior without disrupting workflow—merits attention in future studies [62].

Our study has several limitations that warrant cautious interpretation of the magnitude of the observed benefits. First, a considerable degree of statistical heterogeneity was observed in some analyses. Although we identified several potential sources of heterogeneity, residual variability due to clinical and methodological differences may still persist. Second, evidence of publication bias suggested the small-study effects; however, it was unlikely to have substantial influences on the pooled effect sizes. Third, the number of studies included in some analyses was relatively small, which may limit their conclusions. Fourth, as most included studies were conducted in China and other Asian regions, the generalizability of our findings to other populations may be limited. Fifth, differences in colonoscopy indications among the included studies may introduce bias. Sixth, withdrawal time as a well-established quality indicator in colonoscopy and a critical factor for clinical workflow efficiency was not evaluated in the present study, which limits our ability to assess the impact of AI systems on procedural duration and clinical workflow integration. Finally, the lack of endoscopist blinding introduces a risk of performance and detection bias, though such blinding is often impractical in these types of trials.

In conclusion, we observed beneficial effects of AI-assisted colonoscopy on ADR, SSLDR, AMR, APC, and SSLPC compared with conventional colonoscopy. The modest effect sizes underscore the potential of AI as a valuable adjunctive tool for enhancing human performance in colonoscopic practice. However, its limited impact on SSL detection highlights a critical area for future technological advancement, particularly through training AI systems with larger and more specialized datasets of these lesions. Future research should prioritize investigations into the cost-effectiveness of this technology and its long-term impact on post-colonoscopy CRC incidence in population-based studies.
