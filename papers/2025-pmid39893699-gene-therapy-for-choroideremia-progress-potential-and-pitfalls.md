## Abstract

**INTRODUCTION**: Choroideremia is a rare disease with a significant disease burden. Gene-supplementation methods for choroideremia gene therapy have been the most successful form of gene therapy thus far.

**AREAS COVERED**: The aim of the current review is to provide an overview of current progress of gene therapy trials to date, with a focus on potential and pitfalls of such trials. We propose a novel end point that may be clinically meaningful for obtaining regulatory approval in subsequent clinical trials. Additionally, we offer recommendations for further optimization of surgical techniques.

**EXPERT OPINION**: Lessons learnt from this phase 3 clinical trial, encompassing optimal vector design, delivery techniques, patient selection criteria, and long-term safety profiles can be used in the development of treatments for polygenic retinal disorders, which may necessitate a more nuanced approach due to genetic complexity.

## Full Text

### Introduction

Following the 2017 approval of voretigene neparvovec-rzyl, the first Food and Drug Administration-approved gene replacement therapy in the United States and in the European Union, numerous gene therapy clinical trials to treat other inherited retinal degenerations have been in progress. The most compelling achievements to date have involved gene-supplementation approaches, in which a genetic deficiency is compensated by the sustained local expression of the deficient protein via the use of a recombinant adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector as a means to introduce complementary cDNA, encoding the transgene, into surviving cells. Choroideremia is one such inherited retinal degeneration in which gene augmentation is being tested in multi-institutional gene therapy clinical trials and where clinical trials have been the most advanced [1–8]. The recently published results of the phase III clinical trial assessed the efficacy and safety of subretinal AAV2.REP1 (timrepigene emparvovec) compared to a non-surgical control in individuals with choroideremia over a 12-month period [9]. Although the primary endpoint (a three-line gain in best-corrected visual acuity) was not reached in this pivotal trial, a statistically significant gain in the proportion of patients gaining two lines was achieved, although as a secondary endpoint. Unfortunately, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) defines a clinically significant gain in visual acuity as being at least three lines, whereas two lines would have been an acceptable endpoint for the European Medicines Agency (EMA). Hence, subretinal gene therapy for choroideremia holds great potential as a therapeutic approach and the trial yielded useful clinical insights into the design and characteristics of clinical endpoints that are most appropriate for future therapeutic interventions. The objective of this review is to highlight the key insights gained from this clinical trial in order to inform the design of future clinical trials. The science of gene therapy for retinal diseases continues to advance at a rapid rate, and the invaluable data obtained from treating monogenic disorders such as choroideremia is crucial in the treatment of polygenic diseases.

### Subretinal timrepigene emparvovec in adult men with choroideremia: a randomized phase 3 trial

Visual acuity gains below the predetermined threshold for the primary endpoint can still be of clinical value particularly in a disease with no approved treatment in which preservation of vision rather than increase is the key goal. The treatment groups did not have a sufficient number of subjects who achieved three lines of vision to reach statistical significance, although a positive trend was seen. These findings encompassed all visual acuity endpoints analyzed, including the proportion of participants reporting a two-line improvement, those reporting a mean gain in vision, and those who maintained at least one line of vision. In all parameters measured, the treated eyes outperformed the controls.

It is noteworthy that despite the surgical stress experienced by the treated eyes, namely iatrogenic retinal detachment in thin and degenerate tissues, which would typically result in decreased visual acuity, over the entire patient cohort, the patients who had the surgical intervention fared better overall, with the best results seen in the high dose compared to the control group [9]. The control group experienced a decrease of −2.3 ETDRS letters in best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) in the study eye after 12 months. In comparison, the low dosage group had a decrease of −1.5 ETDRS letters, and the high dose group, a decrease of −0.3 ETDRS letters. The least squares (LS) mean difference (95% confidence interval (CI)) in the change from baseline in study eye BCVA at month 12 between the high-dose group and the control group was a gain of 2.1 (−2.0, 6.2) ETDRS letters, favoring the high dose. The LS mean difference in the change from baseline in study eye BCVA score at 12 months between the low-dose group and the control group was a gain of 0.9 ETDRS letters, favoring the low dose. Although none of the participants in the control group experienced a spontaneous improvement of three lines of vision, the treatment groups did not have enough participants who gained three lines of vision to reach statistical significance. Even at the low dose, the multiplicity of infection still surpasses the threshold of 105 viral particles/cell when targeting RPE, indicating its potential therapeutic effect. It is worth noting that the proportion of patients who gained three lines of vision in a meta-analysis of the phase 1/2 choroideremia trials was more than twice the rate observed in this study [10]. Multiple factors may have contributed to this result.

First, the Phase III study patients had more advanced disease (34–73 ETDRS letters) than those enrolled in earlier trials because of the low BCVA entry requirement, thus their more advanced degeneration might have limited their BCVA gain potential. In a situation where the effect of the measure is compromised due to advanced disease, a larger sample size would be mandatory to demonstrate statistical certainty of the interventional effect. This has typically been of limited concern in trials involving glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy or age-related macular degeneration due to the frequency of these ocular diseases and, hence, the large pool of potential recruits. In rare inherited retinal diseases, such as choroideremia, recruiting such a large number of patients to gain statistical significance is challenging. Interestingly, the power calculation obtained from the data of the Phase III research indicates that a clinical trial with a two-line improvement (p = 0.017) as the main outcome would have led to the FDA approving timrepigene emparvovec as a therapy for patients with choroideremia. A ≥ 10-letter improvement in BCVA from baseline has been deemed clinically relevant from the patient’s perspective, based on scientific advice provided by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) during a workshop [11]. This highlights the importance of endpoints being both clinically and statistically significant. Due to the majority of funding for the Phase III study being provided by US investors, a primary endpoint approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) was selected. The presence of differing clinical endpoints across different regulatory jurisdictions may lead to a scenario where medication approval is determined by geographical location rather than efficacy. Finally, and not discussed in this article, is the fact that investigational medicinal product (IMP) approval is also dependent on having a robust and consistent manufacturing process and this is highly challenging and expensive when making biological therapeutics such as AAV. There will be many rare inherited diseases in which pharmaceutical corporations are simply unable to make a profit against the challenges of meeting somewhat arbitrary manufacturing targets laid down inconsistently across different regulatory authorities around the world.

An additional factor contributing to the challenge of achieving the primary outcome of a three-line gain in this crucial phase 3 trial is the exclusion of patients with early-stage disease and almost normal vision, despite the understanding that they would benefit the most from retinal gene therapy interventions. This occurred as a consequence of the difficulties encountered by investigators in obtaining regulatory approval. First, the threshold for approval predesignated by the FDA as the primary end point was a three-line (15-letter) gain in a significantly greater proportion of patients compared with the control group. Individuals with choroideremia generally retain their visual acuity until the advanced stages of the disease. Consequently, gains in BCVA necessitate the involvement of individuals in advanced stages of the disease whose central vision has dropped to between 35–73 ETDRS letters (to allow headroom to gain 15 letters—88 letters is better than 6/6 or 20/20). This group frequently displays notable dysfunction and degeneration of the central retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), which is the specific cell targeted for transduction in choroideremia. As gene therapy for choroideremia aims to restore REP1 function and prenylation activity to the remaining RPE cells, thereby preventing or slowing RPE cell death, and secondary photoreceptor and choroidal degeneration, dysfunctional RPE may be unable to express the REP1 transgene as efficiently, limiting efficacy. Regrettably, individuals with choroideremia that is very advanced and therefore end-stage are intentionally selected for clinical trials in order to prevent a ceiling effect that hinders the ability to measure gains in BCVA (best corrected visual acuity).

Additionally, it may be argued that it is ethically questionable to expose patients with early disease to the potential risks associated with surgical intervention to demonstrate treatment efficacy. However, the Phase III study results indicate that a higher percentage of individuals in both the high-dose (83%) and low-dose (71%) groups maintained at least one line (equivalent to five letters) of ETDRS acuity compared to the unoperated control group (68%). Furthermore, evidence obtained from phase 1/2 studies have shown that timrepigene emparvovec – based gene therapy increased the visual acuity in a subset of choroideremia patients who underwent treatment [2,4,6,7]. After treatment, the best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) in the study eye improved or remained unchanged for most patients. Among patients with moderate vision loss at the outset of the study (34–73 Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) letters), there was a greater mean improvement in vision at 24 months (5.6 letters) compared to all patients (3.1 letters). Moreover, three patients (9%) achieved and maintained a clinically significant gain of ≥ 15 ETDRS letters at 24 months [10]. Both of these study results provide evidence for the safety of the gene therapy treatment. Furthermore, Aleman and colleagues’ interim findings from an ongoing Phase I/II gene augmentation therapy trial using a subretinal delivery of AAV2-hCHM (NCT02341807; Spark Therapeutics) demonstrated that after two years, 13 out of 15 patients experienced a return of visual acuity to within 15 letters of baseline [12]. Finally, despite the fact that choroideremia patients may have an elevated risk of developing macular holes owing to their thin and fragile retinas, surgery appears to be effective in attaining anatomical closure in such circumstances [13–15].

Investigators encounter a particular obstacle in choroideremia gene therapy due to the difficulty in detecting visual benefits in a short pivotal clinical trial due to the slow progression of retinal degeneration. The duration of current choroideremia trials is five years or less [2–7]. Given that the treatment’s objective is to halt further deterioration, and since the natural history of choroideremia is of an extremely slow degeneration, it may be suggested that patients ought to be monitored for at least 10 years to observe a significant outcome [16]. The costs of conducting clinical trials are further inflated by contract research organization (CRO) intermediaries, making it economically unfeasible to carry out a trial of such a long duration. The manufacture of viral vectors is also costly due to excessive regulation and the necessity for validation of AAV batch production methods. The latter may be quite unnecessary when a single 500 ml batch of drug product could treat over 1,000 patients with a rare single retinal gene disorder who require 0.1 ml each. Investigators are therefore required to demonstrate benefit in short pivotal clinical trials, which are typically 1–2 years in duration. Given that choroideremia degeneration in the control (untreated) eye is slow, patients with late disease are enrolled despite the general consensus that earlier intervention is needed for maximal therapeutic effect.

Lastly, it is of note that choroideremia is a disease of RPE degeneration and is an archetypal aging disease. The notion that choroideremia is a disease that initially causes visual impairment by functional defects and that cell death occurs much later, is critical for gene therapy programs. This is because while the expression of the transgenic REP1 in RPE cells can restore any functional impairment, the effectiveness of gene therapy is contingent upon the capacity of the cells to recover when the gene is replaced. As in the Phase III study, small boosts in retinal function can be demonstrated as seen on microperimetry, however, it is not enough to reverse degeneration and cause anatomical regeneration. The decades-long, chronic membrane traffic defects in the RPE have resulted in the gradual accumulation of lipofuscin-containing vesicles [17]. Such cells have reached a nadir and are beyond the critical point of no return. In contrast, in the case of RPGR-associated dystrophies, gene therapy may result in anatomical reversal as the patients are younger, and with a far more rapid disease progression – there is sudden loss of photoreceptor outer segments and cell death. This pathogenesis explains why X-linked retinitis pigmentosa, caused by pathogenic RPGR variants, is one of the most severe forms of retinitis pigmentosa, with early onset of disease and progression to legal blindness by the third or fourth decade [18,19].

### Choroideremia as an archetypal aging disease

Both age-related disorders and choroideremia are linked to malfunction of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). REP1 enhances the transfer of a geranylgeranyl group by GGTases to the c-terminus of Rab GTPases, which are a superfamily of Ras G-proteins involved in intracellular vesicle trafficking [20–23]. Multiple factors contribute to inefficient prenylation of Rab27a in Rab prenylation diseases [24–26]. Furthermore, REP1 serves an essential role in facilitating the transportation of Rab proteins from the cytoplasm to their designated membrane. Prenylation is the process that imparts hydrophobic properties to Rabs, enabling them to bind to membranes [27]. The fusion of shed outer segment discs from photoreceptors with endosomes and lysosomes occurs in the RPE to create mature phagolysosomes [28], specifically near the basolateral membrane. The acidic pH promotes the degradation of the engulfed shed outer segments.

Given the majority, if not all of CHM mutations are functionally null, vesicle trafficking is impaired, which can subsequently impact cell homeostasis and ultimately result in cell death. Photoreceptors and the RPE are largely postmitotic and experience a significant workload due to the daily production of outer segments by the photoreceptors and the daily degradation of phagocytosed shed outer segments by the RPE. This high traffic burden may render these cell types particularly susceptible to partial defects in membrane trafficking. This hypothesis that there is high metabolic demand on the RPE in choroideremia is supported by the observation that the mid-peripheral retina is affected early in disease and that there is prolonged relative sparing of foveal structure and function [29]. This striking feature of foveal sparing is also commonly observed in late-onset Stargardt disease and central areolar choroidal dystrophy. The variation in metabolic activity across different regions of the retina is one proposed mechanism. The mid-peripheral retina, in particular, has the greatest metabolic demand as it forms a watershed zone where the density of photoreceptors relative to RPE cells is at its maximum.

Chm RPE demonstrated characteristics of prematurely aged RPE in mouse models with Chm KO in the RPE [30]. In addition, Wavre-Shapton and colleagues showed that Chm/Rep1 knockout in mouse RPE cells led to the emergence of aging characteristics in the RPE that resembled certain aspects of age-related macular degeneration phenotypes [31]. The observed changes consisted of reduced numbers of melanosomes in the apical processes, a delay in phagosome degradation, and the intracellular accumulation of lipofuscin-containing deposits.

### Selection of participants with a more preserved retinal area

Considering the challenges highlighted earlier in employing a three-line improvement in standard-luminance, high-contrast best-corrected visual acuity, it is imperative to validate alternative outcome in a condition that currently has no recognized treatment. If an objective structural outcome measure can be proven to be associated with preserved or improved retinal sensitivity, it may be of particular value in clinical trials as a surrogate endpoint. A novel, and potentially more valuable biomarker, that would more easily be met in a small follow-on clinical trial, is the measurement of the ‘smooth zone’ of retinal short-wavelength autofluorescence (SW-AF) – specifically, its preservation, enlargement, or degeneration rate. A parallel phase II clinical trial, funded by the NIHR, examined the effects of gene therapy on patients with early stages of choroideremia. Sub-analysis of patients with a ‘smooth zone’ of retinal autofluorescence [32,33] may be a more appropriate way of assessing slowing of degeneration because this region contains healthy RPE cells that are yet to be compromised by the REP1 deficiency [33]. It appears that as the condition advances, the smooth zone becomes mottled and eventually disappears completely, resulting in vision loss. These distinct appearances have been observed to correlate with the integrity of the ellipsoid zone on optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging, which is a biomarker of the health of the overlying photoreceptors. The study by Stevanovic et al. (2020) demonstrated that smooth AF zones correlated with normal melanosome distribution seen with infrared (IR) imaging and exhibited a predominantly intact ellipsoid zone and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), whilst mottled zones were characterized by loss of the IR melanosome signal and significant disruption of the ellipsoid zone [33].

Patients in the phase III clinical trial exhibited extensive degeneration with sparse surviving regions of RPE, making them unsuitable for the accurate analysis of slowing degeneration. In contrast, preliminary analysis of the NIHR-funded clinical trial (Regenerate) in younger patients indicated that this ‘smooth zone’ may remain preserved following gene therapy [34] (Figure 1). To ascertain the clinical significance of the ‘smooth zone’ as an anatomical endpoint, further examination of the smooth zone in early-stage patients is required. The smooth zone area on SW-AF could serve as a functionally validated structural biomarker that is applicable to individuals with early disease, where measurement of visual acuity is restricted owing to a ceiling effect. Furthermore, since the restoration of prenylation activity through transduction and relies on the presence of surviving RPE and photoreceptor cells [30], it may be more effective to target the smooth zone using subretinal delivery. The presence of structural degeneration and functional loss in the mottled zone suggests that the RPE is under stress, making it more challenging to restore its normal function. Additional research must be conducted to fully characterize the natural history of changes in the smooth zone area and sensitivity with time. A comprehensive investigation of retinal function and anatomy, using microperimetry, OCT, and near-infrared autofluorescence imaging, needs to be performed.

The mottled zone surrounding the smooth zone island exhibits a heterogeneous autofluorescence appearance. ### Optimization of surgical techniques

Currently, the state of the art in gene therapy for treating diseases of the outer retina and choroid involves the delivery of adeno-associated viral (AAV) vectors into the subretinal space. This allows for successful transduction of diseased photoreceptors and retinal pigment epithelium. Although the intravitreal route may be considered as an alternative method, the vitreous and internal limiting membrane serve as a physical obstacle for targeting photoreceptors and RPE. Intravitreal injections, which are commonly employed in ophthalmology due to their high level of safety and effectiveness, are unable to overcome this particular impediment. Moreover, the administration of AAV vectors through the intravitreal route will lead to a heightened immune response compared to the subretinal technique as dilution in the 4.2 ml vitreous chamber could necessitate a 2–3 log higher concentration of vector compared to 0.1 ml. Even more if one assumes that the retinal tissue will impair diffusion of AAV particles through it to the RPE. The third delivery route, the suprachoroidal route, which has demonstrated reliability in animal models with small eye, might be considered in cases when a less invasive method is preferred [35]. However, the choroid in choroideremia is pathologically thin and completely absent in places. Hence, in some cases this would be no different to a subretinal route. Furthermore, the suprachoroidal space is not an immune-privileged environment, and the presence of the highly vascular choriocapillaris layer facilitates the rapid clearance of soluble drug products such as AAV, impeding efficient transduction of the retina [36].

A safe and reproducible subretinal surgical technique [5,37] has been developed since 1994 when Li et al. demonstrated the transduction of outer retinal layers after subretinal delivery of a replication-deficient adenoviral vector in murine retina [38]. The two-step approach employed by MacLaren et al. and Fischer et al., consisting of inducing a BSS subretinal bleb and secondly injecting the drug subretinally, is of special relevance to choroideremia patients. This is because the retinal atrophy in these individuals poses challenges in detaching the retina. Recent adaptive optics images have shown that the cone mosaic remains intact after subretinal administration of AAV2-hCHM, giving compelling evidence for the safety of subretinal injections for gene augmentation in choroideremia. Although there is consensus that a successful surgery is key for the clinical effectiveness of subretinal gene therapy, the surgeon’s manual for administration of voretigene neparvovec-rzyl intentionally gives general, broad directions with limited specifics to allow for surgical discretion. Education on recommended techniques for subretinal injection is necessary, and it is worth mentioning that initially only two surgeons conducted RPE65 gene therapy in the Phase 3 clinical study. The use of intraoperative optical coherence tomography (e.g. using a Zeiss Rescan 7000, Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany; Haag-Streit intraoperative OCT system, Haag-Streit, Switzerland; Leica Proveo microscope with integrated EnFocus OCT, Leica Microsystems, Danaher, Washington DC, U.S.A.; or the Duke swept- source microscope-integrated OCT system) [35] enhances surgical precision and is hence, mandated in any subretinal gene therapy surgery. This type of imaging offers real-time visualization of subretinal drug delivery and allows surgeons to quantify and assess the success of the procedure. The intraoperative OCT serves two particular purposes in subretinal gene therapy surgery: monitoring the stretching of the thinned choroideremia fovea and monitoring the injection of fluid into the retina and beneath the retina. Continual endeavors are being undertaken to advance surgical robotics with the aim of enhancing the precision of subretinal injections. Vitreoretinal surgery for macular hemorrhage, involving the use of a high-precision robot to deliver TPA medication to the subretinal area has recently proven to be both effective and safe [39].

### CRISPR DNA editing

Although gene augmentation techniques have advanced the furthest clinically, the recent study demonstrating proof of principle of DNA editing using the CRISPR/Cas9 (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats/CRISPR-associated nuclease 9) system in patients with CEP290-associated inherited retinal degeneration has expanded the arsenal of potential treatments for inherited retinal gene diseases [1]. This is an appealing therapeutic approach as correcting the genomic copy of the gene may provide greater assurance of achieving and maintaining the desired level of gene expression. This is because the gene would be subject to regulation by its normal transcriptional regulation and epigenetic environment. However, this approach requires further elucidation due to concern regarding the potential for harm due to unintended off-target effects from the ubiquitously expressed Cas9 protein. In addition, it is worth noting that the majority of choroideremia mutations are unique within families, lacking a prevalent mutation that can be readily targeted. For instance, Han et al revealed that the five most frequently occurring pathogenic variants account for only 11% of all pathogenic variants [40]. Furthermore, given the small size of the CHM cDNA (1.96 kb), choroideremia is well-suited for adeno-associated viral (AAV) retinal gene therapy. Hence, whilst CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing may be an attractive option for particular choroideremia-causing mutations, it is pertinent to recognize that DNA gene editing may provide only minimal advantages compared to gene replacement.

### Expert opinion

Choroideremia is a rare disease with a considerable disease burden. Dong et al report that individuals with choroideremia exhibit a greater average frequency of hospital admissions, outpatient consultations, and emergency department visits compared to those without the condition. The disease also leads to significantly higher all-cause total annualized costs compared to a control cohort across age – these entail expenses unrelated to choroideremia.

Subretinal delivery of a functioning CHM copy using an adeno-associated virus vector has been employed in a pivotal phase 3 clinical trial. The trial demonstrated significant clinical improvements; nonetheless, it failed to fulfill the requirements for regulatory approval. Although the primary endpoint (a three-line gain in best-corrected visual acuity) was not reached in this landmark trial, a statistically significant gain in the proportion of patients gaining two lines was achieved, although as a secondary endpoint. The proportion of patients who gained three lines of vision in a meta-analysis of the phase 1/2 choroideremia trials was more than twice the rate observed in this study.

Numerous factors may have influenced this outcome. The Phase III study participants had more advanced disease due to the low best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) admission criterion. Their more advanced degeneration may have constrained their potential for BCVA improvement because of degeneration at or around the fovea. In addition, the efficacy of the gene therapy treatment may be hindered by dysfunction of the retinal pigment epithelium. Gene therapy for choroideremia attempts to restore REP1 function and prenylation activity in the remaining retinal pigment epithelium cells (RPE); however, dysfunctional RPE may exhibit diminished effectiveness in expressing the REP1 transgene, hence reducing therapeutic efficacy. Individuals with early-stage disease and almost normal vision were excluded from this clinical trial, despite the recognition that they would derive the greatest benefit from retinal gene therapy interventions. Furthermore, the slow progression of retinal degeneration in choroideremia creates challenges in determining the presence of benefits over a short clinical trials. The expenses associated with conducting clinical trials and manufacturing viral vectors render prolonged trials economically unviable. Due to the aforementioned reasons, it is essential to validate alternative outcomes in a condition that is currently incurable.

We propose a novel, objective structural outcome measure that could serve as a more clinically relevant endpoint for securing regulatory approval in a small follow-on clinical trial. This valuable biomarker involves measurement of the ‘smooth zone’ of retinal short-wavelength autofluorescence (SW-AF), focusing on its preservation, expansion, or degeneration rate. The ‘smooth zone’ of retinal autofluorescence may serve as an improved approach for evaluating the deceleration of degeneration, as this area comprises healthy retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells that remain as yet unaffected by REP1 deficiency. As the disease progresses, the smooth area becomes mottled and ultimately vanishes, leading to vision loss.

The findings from this phase 3 clinical trial, encompassing optimal vector design, delivery techniques, and patient selection criteria, can be applied to the development of treatments for polygenic retinal disorders, which may require more nuanced approached due to their genetic complexity. Moreover, the data obtained from these trials, comprising patient responses and long-term safety profiles, can inform risk assessments and therapeutic strategies.
